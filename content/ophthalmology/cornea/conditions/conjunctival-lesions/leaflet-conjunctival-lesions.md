This leaflet is for people who have a growth or spot on the conjunctiva, the thin clear layer covering the white of the eye. It explains the common types, and how a growth called ocular surface squamous neoplasia (OSSN) is treated, including with chemotherapy eye drops. There is a separate leaflet on pterygium. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## Common, harmless growths

**Pinguecula.** This is a small, raised, white or yellowish spot on the white of the eye, next to the cornea (the clear window at the front of the eye). It is caused by long-term exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light. Pingueculae become more common with age, and by the age of 70 most people have them. They usually affect both eyes and do not affect vision [1].

- A pinguecula is harmless, and is not a threat to your sight or general health [2].
- Wear a hat and sunglasses in sunny weather. Lubricating drops can help if it feels irritated [1].
- Removing a pinguecula is usually considered cosmetic, so it is rarely done on the NHS [1].

**Conjunctival naevus (mole).** This is a brown spot on the white of the eye. It may be present from birth or appear later, and is the most common type of pigmented spot on the conjunctiva [3]. Most stay the same and are harmless [3, 4]. Very rarely, a naevus can turn into a melanoma (a type of cancer) [3, 4]. In a large US study, this happened in {{risk.cornea.conjunctival_lesions.naevus_to_melanoma}} people [5].

- Your optometrist or eye team may take a photograph, so that any changes can be spotted [4].
- Tell your optometrist or eye team if a naevus gets bigger, changes colour, becomes raised, or develops new blood vessels [4].

**Primary acquired melanosis (PAM)** is a brown patch on the conjunctiva. Some types can turn into melanoma, so a small sample (biopsy) may be taken to check it [4, 6].

## Ocular surface squamous neoplasia (OSSN)

OSSN is a group of conditions in which abnormal cells grow on the surface of the eye. It ranges from pre-cancerous changes, called conjunctival intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), to a type of cancer called squamous cell carcinoma [6, 7].

- **What it looks like:** usually a pale, fleshy lump on the surface of the eye, next to the cornea. It can grow onto the cornea. If it is large, it can cause grittiness, watering and blurred vision [6]. Small growths may cause no symptoms, and may be found at a routine eye test [8].
- **Causes:** sun damage is the main cause. It is also more likely in people with fair skin, older people, and people with a weakened immune system, for example because of HIV or medicines that suppress the immune system [6, 8].
- **How common:** OSSN is uncommon in the UK. It usually affects older people, and is more common in men [9, 10].

It can be hard to tell pre-cancerous changes from cancer just by looking, so the growth, or a sample of it, is usually sent to the laboratory to confirm the diagnosis [6].

## How is OSSN treated?

The main treatments are surgery and chemotherapy eye drops. They are often used together [6, 8].

- **Surgery.** The growth is removed in a short operation. This is usually done while you are awake, with sedation, and you can normally go home the same day [8]. Freezing treatment (cryotherapy) may be used at the same time [6].
- **Chemotherapy eye drops,** such as mitomycin C or fluorouracil (5-FU), may be used after surgery, or sometimes instead of it [6, 7, 8]. The drops treat the whole surface of the eye, including abnormal cells too small to see [11]. They only reach the surface layers, so they do not work for growths that have spread deeper [11].

## Using chemotherapy eye drops safely

Chemotherapy eye drops can harm healthy cells and irritate the skin, so they must be handled carefully [12]. Your eye team and pharmacy will give you detailed instructions. These usually include:

- **Gloves:** wear disposable gloves when putting the drops in, and protect the skin around your eye with a barrier cream such as petroleum jelly [12].
- **Storage:** keep the drops in the fridge, in a container, away from food [12].
- **After each drop:** close your eye and press gently on the inner corner for a short time [12]. This may help protect the tear ducts [11].
- **Disposal:** put used gloves and tissues in a sealed bag. Put used bottles in the special cytotoxic waste bin you are given. Never pour the drops down a sink or toilet [12].
- **Stains:** mitomycin C drops are coloured and can stain clothing and furniture [12].
- **Pregnancy:** tell your doctor if you are pregnant, trying to become pregnant or breastfeeding [12]. For fluorouracil, both men and women are advised to use contraception during treatment and for three months afterwards [11]. Ask your eye team what applies to your drops.

Mitomycin C is not licensed for use as an eye drop, but it has been used in specialist eye clinics for many years [12].

**Side effects.** While you are using the drops, your eye is likely to be red, irritated and sore, and may water or produce sticky discharge [11, 12]. These usually settle after the course has finished [12]. The drops can sometimes block the tear ducts, causing a permanently watery eye [11].

## Follow-up

OSSN can come back, sometimes months or years later, so you will need regular check-ups, often for life [11]. In a UK specialist centre, OSSN came back within a year in about {{risk.cornea.conjunctival_lesions.ossn_recurrence_1y_uk}} people [10]. Treatment in a specialist centre can still give good results if it does [10].

## When to get help

**If you are using chemotherapy eye drops,** contact us straight away if you have [12]:

- severe redness of the eye or eyelids
- itching or swelling of the eyelids
- a drooping eyelid
- severe sensitivity to light
- eye pain lasting two to three days or more, or sharp, severe pain that painkillers do not help

**At any time,** contact your eye team if a growth or spot on your eye is getting bigger, changing colour, or developing new blood vessels [4].
