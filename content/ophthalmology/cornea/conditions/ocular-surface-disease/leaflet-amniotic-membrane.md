This leaflet is for people who have been offered amniotic membrane treatment for their eye. It explains what amniotic membrane is, why it is used, how it is put on the eye, how well it works and what the risks are. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is amniotic membrane?

Amniotic membrane is part of the placenta [1]. It is used in eye surgery as a graft and as a dressing for the surface of the eye [1]. It has properties that support healing [2].

## Where does it come from?

The membrane is donated by mothers giving birth by planned caesarean section [1]. Donating is completely voluntary and does not interfere with the birth of the baby [1]. Before the birth, a trained donation nurse asks the mother about her medical history and takes a blood sample [1]. In the UK, tissue donations must be voluntary and unpaid [3].

## Why might I need it?

Amniotic membrane is used to help the surface of the eye when it is damaged and is not healing, or needs repair [4]. Reasons include:

- a sore patch on the surface of the cornea that has not healed (a persistent epithelial defect) [4]
- chemical or heat injury to the eye [4]
- a painful, swollen cornea (bullous keratopathy) [4]
- repairing the surface of the eye during surgery [4]
- damage to the stem cells that keep the cornea healthy (limbal stem cell deficiency) [4]
- severe loss of feeling in the cornea (neurotrophic keratitis) [5]

It can also be added to treatment for a corneal infection [6].

## How is it put on the eye?

There are different ways, and your eye team will tell you which is planned for you:

- **Stitched in place in the operating theatre.** This covers the whole surface of the eye, but takes longer and often needs a general anaesthetic [7].
- **Glued in place.** This is quicker and less invasive than stitching [7].
- **Held on the eye in clinic,** under a special contact lens or ring, without stitches. This is quick and is designed to be comfortable, but does not cover the whole surface of the eye [7].

In a UK study, using a bandage contact lens at the end of the procedure was linked with better results [4].

## How long does it stay on?

A membrane laid on the surface of the eye stays there until it is removed or dissolves on its own [2]. In a large study of membranes placed in clinic, they typically stayed on the eye for about {{risk.cornea.amniotic_membrane.days_in_place_clinic}} [8]. Some people need more than one treatment: in one large study, the treatment was repeated in about {{risk.cornea.amniotic_membrane.repeat_treatment}} eyes [9].

## How well does it work?

How well it works depends on the reason it is used:

- In a UK national audit of persistent sore patches on the cornea, the surface had healed and was stable after three months in about {{risk.cornea.amniotic_membrane.ped_healed_3m}} eyes [4]. The audit found results were generally less good than earlier reports had suggested [4].
- When added to treatment for a corneal infection, it helped the cornea heal about {{risk.cornea.amniotic_membrane.keratitis_faster_healing}} sooner on average. This evidence is of low quality [6].
- For chemical or heat burns, there is uncertain evidence that it helps moderate burns. It has not been shown to help severe burns [10].
- It has been reported to help with pain [2].

## What are the other options?

Depending on your eye, other options may include lubricating eye drops, antibiotic eye drops to prevent infection, and closing the eyelids partly or fully with stitches (tarsorrhaphy) [5]. In a small trial of people with corneal ulcers caused by loss of feeling, tarsorrhaphy or a bandage contact lens worked about as well as amniotic membrane [11]. Your eye specialist will talk through the options that suit you.

## Is the donated tissue safe?

Donors are carefully selected, and their blood is tested for infections [1, 12]. Organisations that supply tissue for treatment in the UK must be licensed, under rules set by the Human Tissue Authority, and the tissue must be traceable from the donor to the person who receives it [3].

All human tissue carries some risk of passing on disease [12]. There is no test for variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (vCJD). The risk of passing it on through tissue is thought to be very low, but it cannot be ruled out [12].

## What are the risks?

- **Discomfort.** About {{risk.cornea.amniotic_membrane.discomfort_clinic_device}} people had discomfort from a membrane held on the eye with a ring in clinic [13].
- **The membrane coming loose or moving.** A small collection of blood can form under the membrane and may need to be drained if it is uncomfortable or pushes the membrane out of place [2].
- **A small lump (granuloma)** can form around stitches [2].
- **Infection.** In a large study, about {{risk.cornea.amniotic_membrane.infection}} people treated with membrane from a tissue supplier developed an infection [14].
- **The eye not healing,** which may mean a repeat treatment or a different treatment [4, 9].

## Your records

A record is kept of the membrane used in your treatment, so that it can be traced back to the donor if ever needed. Tissue suppliers must keep these records for at least 30 years [3].
