This leaflet is for people who have been told they have epithelial basement membrane dystrophy, usually called EBMD. It explains what it is, the problems it can cause, how they are treated, and why it matters if you are thinking about cataract or laser eye surgery. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is EBMD?

The cornea is the clear window at the front of the eye. Its surface layer of cells (the epithelium) sits on a thin base layer called the basement membrane, which holds it in place. In EBMD, this base layer is abnormal, so the surface cells do not stick down as well as they should [1].

An eye specialist can see small patterns on the cornea that look like maps, dots or fingerprints. This is why EBMD is also called map-dot-fingerprint dystrophy, or anterior basement membrane dystrophy [1]. It is one of the most common corneal conditions [2], and it often affects both eyes [3].

It usually shows up in adult life [1]. Although it is called a dystrophy, in most people it is not inherited, and it is thought to be a wear-and-tear change [1]. A few families with an inherited form have been described [3].

## What problems can it cause?

Many people with EBMD have no symptoms at all [1, 4]. When it does cause problems, there are two main types [1, 4]:

- **Painful attacks (recurrent erosions).** Because the surface does not stick down well, it can tear away, usually when you first open your eyes in the morning. This causes sudden pain, redness, watering, sensitivity to light and blurred vision, which can last from minutes to hours [2, 5]. EBMD is the most common cause of recurrent erosions: in a large US study, about {{risk.cornea.ebmd.share_of_recurrent_erosion}} eyes with recurrent erosions had EBMD [6].
- **Blurred or distorted vision.** An uneven surface can cause blurring, or double or "ghost" images in one eye [1].

The pattern on the cornea can change over time [1].

## Treatment for painful attacks

The treatment is the same as for other types of recurrent erosion. There is a separate leaflet on recurrent corneal erosion, which explains it in more detail.

- **Lubricants.** Use lubricating drops or gel during the day and ointment at night, and keep using them for at least three months after your last attack [7].
- **A procedure** may be offered if attacks keep happening despite lubricants [5, 7].

## Procedures

If EBMD keeps causing attacks, or is affecting your vision, a procedure to remove the loose surface layer may help. The loose surface is removed so that a new, smoother surface can grow back [7, 8]. There are different ways of doing this:

- **Removing the loose surface,** sometimes with gentle polishing of the layer beneath (diamond burr polishing) [7, 9]. In a trial in people with recurrent erosions, adding polishing meant attacks came back less often than removing the surface alone [10].
- **PTK laser treatment** (phototherapeutic keratectomy), in which a laser smooths the layer beneath [7, 11].

Research has not shown clearly which of these works best [7, 10].

**Possible risks:**

- **EBMD can come back.** In one study of people treated mainly for blurred vision, the chance of EBMD coming back within five years was about {{risk.cornea.ebmd.debridement_recurrence_5y}} [8]. The procedure can be repeated if needed.
- **Haze.** About {{risk.cornea.ebmd.debridement_haze}} eyes in the same study developed some haze in the cornea [8]. Haze after PTK is usually mild and tends to fade, but it can be permanent in a small number of people [11].
- **A change in your glasses prescription,** especially after PTK [11].
- **Infection.** While the surface is healing, the eye is more open to infection [10]. See "When to get help" below.

## Cataract surgery and EBMD

Before cataract surgery, the eye is measured to choose the strength of the lens implant that replaces your natural lens. An uneven surface caused by EBMD can make these measurements less accurate, so the lens chosen may leave your eye more out of focus than planned [12, 13].

If EBMD is affecting the centre of your cornea, your eye team may suggest treating it first, and repeating the measurements once the surface has settled [12]. In one study, doing this changed the calculated lens strength in about {{risk.cornea.ebmd.cataract_lens_power_change}} eyes [12].

**If you are planning cataract surgery, tell your surgeon that you have EBMD.**

## Laser eye surgery and EBMD

EBMD can cause problems during and after some types of laser eye surgery, because the surface layer can come loose [14, 15]. Some clinics use a surface laser treatment instead in people with more than mild EBMD [15].

**If you are thinking about laser eye surgery, tell the clinic that you have EBMD.**
