This leaflet is for people who have a pterygium, and for people who have been offered surgery to remove one. It explains what a pterygium is, the options for treating it, and what to expect from surgery. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is a pterygium?

A pterygium is a triangular thickening of the conjunctiva, the thin clear layer over the white of the eye. It grows from the white of the eye onto the edge of the cornea, the clear window at the front of the eye, usually on the side nearest the nose [1]. A pterygium itself is not a cancer [2].

## What causes it?

A pterygium is caused by years of exposure to strong sunlight, particularly ultraviolet (UV) light. It is also more likely in people who spend a lot of time outdoors in dry, dusty conditions [1]. It is much more common in sunnier countries nearer the equator [1]. Worldwide, about {{risk.cornea.pterygium.prevalence_world}} people have one, but this varies widely between countries [3]. Wearing sunglasses is linked with a lower chance of getting one [3].

## What are the symptoms?

A pterygium can make the eye red, irritated, dry or gritty [1, 2]. If it grows onto the cornea, it can pull on it and change its shape (astigmatism), which can blur your vision [1]. Some people have no symptoms, but do not like how it looks [1, 2].

## Do I need treatment?

Not always. Your optometrist or eye doctor will measure its size so that any growth can be checked [1]. Lubricating eye drops or ointment may be enough to control irritation. If it is inflamed, a short course of steroid eye drops may help [1]. Drops can ease the symptoms, but only surgery removes a pterygium [2]. A pterygium may slowly grow, but waiting to have surgery later does not make surgery less likely to succeed [2].

Surgery may be offered if the pterygium is growing towards the centre of the cornea and threatening your vision, if the inflammation cannot be controlled [1], or if it is causing symptoms or you are unhappy with how it looks [2].

## What does surgery involve?

- The pterygium is removed [2].
- The bare area is covered with a small piece of your own conjunctiva, usually taken from under your upper eyelid. This is called a conjunctival autograft [2]. Covering the area this way makes the pterygium much less likely to come back (see below) [2, 4].
- The surgery is usually done under local anaesthetic, with numbing drops or a small injection around the eye, so you are awake but should not feel pain. You go home the same day [2].
- Your eye will be covered with a pad until the next day [2].

## Will it come back?

- Without a graft, a pterygium has been reported to come back in more than {{risk.cornea.pterygium.recurrence_bare}} people [4].
- With a graft of your own conjunctiva, UK hospital information gives a figure of about {{risk.cornea.pterygium.recurrence_uk_leaflet}} [2]. In one large study where the graft was held with glue, it came back in about {{risk.cornea.pterygium.recurrence_glue_series}} eyes [6].
- A graft of your own conjunctiva is better at stopping the pterygium coming back than a graft of amniotic membrane [4, 7].
- Holding the graft with tissue glue rather than stitches may make the pterygium less likely to come back and shortens the operation, but may lead to more problems with the graft. This evidence is of low certainty [8].

**Mitomycin.** Some surgeons use a medicine called mitomycin during surgery to lower the chance of the pterygium coming back. It has been linked with serious problems, such as thinning of the white of the eye, which are more likely with stronger or longer use [7]. {{site.pterygium.mitomycin}}

## What are the risks of surgery?

In a large study of pterygium surgery with a glued graft [6]:

- about {{risk.cornea.pterygium.bleeding_surface}} eyes had some bleeding under the surface of the eye, which looks red but clears
- about {{risk.cornea.pterygium.graft_swelling}} grafts swelled
- about {{risk.cornea.pterygium.graft_shrinking}} grafts shrank back a little
- about {{risk.cornea.pterygium.graft_loss}} grafts were lost

Other risks include:

- **infection,** which is uncommon, and less likely because antibiotic drops are used after surgery [2]
- **a faint scar on the cornea** where the pterygium was, which does not usually affect vision [2]
- **a change in your glasses prescription,** because the shape of the cornea can improve after a large pterygium is removed [2]
- **inflammation that lasts longer,** which may need steroid drops for longer [2]
- **the pterygium coming back** (see above)

## After surgery

- Your eye will be painful and sensitive to light for the first few days, and your vision may be a little blurred [2].
- Your eye will look very red. This can take up to four weeks to settle, sometimes a little longer [2].
- Painkillers such as paracetamol or ibuprofen and cold compresses can help. Your eye should feel much more comfortable after a week [2].
