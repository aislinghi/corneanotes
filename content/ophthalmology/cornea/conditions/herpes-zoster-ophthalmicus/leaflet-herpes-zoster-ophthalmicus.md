This leaflet is for people who have shingles on the forehead, scalp or around the eye, called herpes zoster ophthalmicus. It explains what it is, why your eye needs checking, how it is treated, and what to look out for. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is it?

Shingles is caused by the chickenpox virus. After you have had chickenpox, the virus stays in your body and can become active again years later [1, 2]. Shingles becomes more common with age and in people whose immune system is weakened [1]. About {{risk.cornea.herpes_zoster.lifetime_risk}} people get shingles at some point in their life [1].

When shingles affects the forehead, scalp and eye area, usually on one side only, it is called herpes zoster ophthalmicus [3]. This happens in about {{risk.cornea.herpes_zoster.share_ophthalmic}} cases of shingles [1]. In some people only the skin around the eye is affected; in others, the eye itself is affected too [3].

## What are the symptoms?

- tingling, itching or burning of the skin for one to three days before a rash appears [3]
- a painful rash on one side of the forehead or scalp, which turns into blisters and then scabs [2, 3]
- swollen eyelids, which can be severe [3]

If the eye itself is affected, it may be red and painful, feel gritty, be sensitive to light, and your vision may be blurred [3].

**A rash on the nose.** If the rash reaches the tip, side or root of your nose, the eye is more likely to be affected: in studies, this happened in about {{risk.cornea.herpes_zoster.eye_involved_with_nose_rash}} people with a nose rash, compared with about {{risk.cornea.herpes_zoster.eye_involved_without_nose_rash}} without [4]. A rash that does not reach the nose does not rule out eye problems [4].

## Get treatment quickly

Get an urgent GP appointment, or help from NHS 111, if you have shingles and the rash is on or near your eye or nose, or your vision has changed [2]. Antiviral tablets work best if you start them within three days of the rash appearing [2]. Starting them early makes eye problems less likely and lessens the pain [5].

## How is it treated?

- **Antiviral tablets,** usually for 7 to 10 days [3].
- **Steroid eye drops,** if there is inflammation inside the eye. These often need to be reduced very slowly [3]. Do not stop them suddenly unless your eye team tells you to.
- Other eye drops may be needed, for example to lower the pressure in the eye [3].

The choice of medicine is made by your doctor.

## How might my eye be affected?

The following figures come from a large hospital study of people with herpes zoster ophthalmicus [6, 7, 8, 9]:

- **Inflammation inside the eye (uveitis)** affected about {{risk.cornea.herpes_zoster.uveitis}} people. It usually started about 10 days after the rash, so eye problems can begin after the rash has started to get better [7, 5].
- **Eye problems came back** at least once in about {{risk.cornea.herpes_zoster.recurrence}} people, often in the weeks after steroid drops were stopped [8].
- **Loss of feeling in the cornea,** which can lead to damage to its surface, affected about {{risk.cornea.herpes_zoster.reduced_corneal_feeling}} people [9, 3].
- **Vision:** about {{risk.cornea.herpes_zoster.moderate_vision_loss}} eyes had moderate loss of vision, and about {{risk.cornea.herpes_zoster.severe_vision_loss}} had severe loss of vision, mostly from scarring of the cornea [6].

## Pain after shingles

Some people have nerve pain in the area of the rash that lasts after the rash has healed. This is called post-herpetic neuralgia [10]. It is more common in older people: about {{risk.cornea.herpes_zoster.phn_60_64}} people aged 60 to 64, and about {{risk.cornea.herpes_zoster.phn_85}} people aged 85 and over, still have pain three months later [1]. It usually gets better, but this can take from a few months to over a year [10]. Your GP can prescribe medicine for the pain and, if needed, refer you to a pain specialist [10]. Antiviral tablets have not been shown to prevent this pain [11].

## Can I pass it on?

You cannot pass shingles on, but people who have not had chickenpox (or the chickenpox vaccine) can catch chickenpox from you while the rash is weeping [2]. Until the rash has dried out [2]:

- cover it with loose clothing or a non-sticky dressing
- avoid anyone pregnant who has not had chickenpox, people with a weakened immune system, and babies under one month old
- stay off work or school if the rash is weeping and cannot be covered

## The shingles vaccine

The shingles vaccine is offered on the NHS to people [12]:

- when they turn 65, and to everyone aged 70 to 79
- aged 18 and over with a severely weakened immune system

You can get shingles more than once, so the vaccine is still worth having if you have had shingles before [12]. The vaccine greatly reduces the chance of getting shingles [13], and a large US study found it prevented about {{risk.cornea.herpes_zoster.vaccine_hzo}} cases of shingles affecting the eye that would otherwise have happened [14]. One study suggested that in people who have already had shingles in the eye, it may be more likely to come back after vaccination [15], so ask your eye team about the timing if this applies to you.

Contact us straight away if you have a red or painful eye, sensitivity to light, blurred vision or new floaters [3].
