This leaflet is for people with allergic conjunctivitis, an allergy affecting the eyes. It explains what causes it, what you can do, and the treatments available. It also explains two less common but more serious allergic eye conditions. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is allergic conjunctivitis?

Allergic conjunctivitis happens when your eyes react to something you are allergic to, such as pollen [1, 2]. The reaction makes the eyes red, itchy and watery [1]. It is not catching [3].

- **Seasonal allergic conjunctivitis** is the part of hay fever that affects the eyes. It is usually caused by pollen, most often grass pollen, which is highest in the summer [1]. It affects about {{risk.cornea.allergic_conjunctivitis.seasonal_adults}} adults [1].
- **Perennial allergic conjunctivitis** happens all year round, usually because of an allergy to things like house dust mites or animals [1, 2].

It is more common in people who have other allergies, or allergic conditions such as asthma or eczema [1, 2].

## What are the symptoms?

It usually affects both eyes [2]. Symptoms include [2]:

- red, itchy, watery eyes
- sore eyes
- swollen eyelids
- blurry vision, from the extra tears

You may also have hay fever symptoms such as sneezing or a runny nose [2]. Symptoms can carry on for months [2].

## What can I do?

**Avoid the things that trigger your allergy where you can.** For pollen [4]:

- wear wraparound sunglasses or a wide-brimmed hat when you go out
- shower and change your clothes after you have been outside
- keep windows and doors shut as much as possible
- vacuum regularly and dust with a damp cloth
- avoid cutting or walking on grass
- do not dry clothes outside

**Ease your symptoms:**

- **Do not rub your eyes.** It makes the symptoms worse [2].
- **Cool compress.** Hold a cold flannel on your eyes for a few minutes [3].
- **Lubricating eye drops** may help with discomfort, but do not stop the allergy [2].
- **Contact lenses:** wear glasses instead until your symptoms settle [2, 5]. If you wear contact lenses, check whether any drops you use are safe to use with them [5].

## Treatments

A pharmacist can give advice and suggest eye drops or antihistamines [3]. For children under 2, you will need a prescription from a GP [3].

- **Anti-allergy eye drops** (antihistamine or mast cell stabiliser drops) reduce symptoms in the short term and are generally safe [1, 6]. Some need to be used every day and may take up to two weeks to work [2]. Research has not shown that any one type works better than the others [6].
- **Antihistamine tablets** can also help, and usually help hay fever symptoms in the nose too [1]. Some can make you sleepy, so ask your pharmacist about non-drowsy ones [4].
- **Steroid eye drops** are only used for severe cases. They work well but can have serious side effects, so they need a prescription and careful monitoring [2, 5].

See your GP if your symptoms are getting worse, or do not improve with treatments from the pharmacy [4].

## More serious allergic eye conditions

Two less common allergic conditions can affect the cornea, the clear window at the front of the eye, and need care from an eye specialist:

- **Vernal keratoconjunctivitis** mainly affects children, especially boys, usually starting before the age of 10. Most have other allergies such as eczema or asthma. It usually settles at puberty [7].
- **Atopic keratoconjunctivitis** usually starts in the late teens or early adulthood, in people who have asthma and eczema. It needs long-term treatment to prevent scarring of the cornea [8].

Both can threaten sight if not treated [7, 8].

**Eye rubbing and keratoconus.** Rubbing your eyes and having allergies are both linked to keratoconus, a condition in which the cornea becomes thin and cone-shaped [9]. This is another reason to avoid rubbing your eyes and to treat eye allergies well.

## When to get help urgently

Get an urgent GP appointment or help from NHS 111 if you have eye pain, sensitivity to light, changes in your vision, or very red eyes [3]. These can be signs of a more serious problem.
