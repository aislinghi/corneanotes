This leaflet is for people who have peripheral ulcerative keratitis, usually called PUK. It explains what it is, why it matters for your general health as well as your eyes, how it is treated, and why regular follow-up is important. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is PUK?

PUK is inflammation at the edge of the cornea, the clear window at the front of the eye. It causes a sore (ulcer) and thinning of the cornea in that area [1]. It is uncommon [1].

The main symptoms are a red, painful eye, sensitivity to light and blurred vision [1]. It usually affects one eye, but in a UK study both eyes were affected in about {{risk.cornea.puk.both_eyes_uk}} people [2].

## What causes it?

When it is not caused by an infection, PUK is linked to an autoimmune disease in about {{risk.cornea.puk.autoimmune_share}} cases [1]. In an autoimmune disease, the body's defences attack its own tissues. The most common link is rheumatoid arthritis, followed by vasculitis, a group of conditions causing inflammation of the blood vessels [1, 3]. Infections can also cause it [1]. Sometimes no cause is found [1].

## Why does it matter for my general health?

PUK can be the first sign of vasculitis affecting other parts of the body [4]. In one study, PUK appeared before or at the same time as the diagnosis of an autoimmune disease in about {{risk.cornea.puk.first_sign}} people [5]. Untreated, these conditions can be serious for your general health as well as your eyes [4]. This is why your eye team will look for an underlying cause, and why treating it matters.

## Tests

Your eye team will ask about your general health and examine you. You may need blood and urine tests, and sometimes other tests such as a chest X-ray, to look for an underlying condition [6]. A sample may be taken from the eye to rule out infection [1].

## How is it treated?

PUK needs prompt treatment.

- **Treating the underlying cause** is the first priority [1].
- **Medicines to calm the immune system,** usually steroid tablets together with another immune-calming medicine, are used to control the inflammation. The steroid is then reduced slowly, while the other medicine is continued to stop PUK coming back [1]. In one study, starting these medicines within four weeks led to quicker control, fewer flare-ups and better vision [7].
- **Eye and rheumatology teams working together.** Your eye team and a rheumatology (joints and immune system) team will usually look after you together [6].
- **Lubricating eye drops** help protect the surface of the eye [6].
- **Surgery,** if the cornea becomes very thin or a small hole forms. Options include sealing it with a special tissue glue and a contact lens, a graft of amniotic membrane, or a patch graft of donor cornea [1, 6].

## Medicines that calm the immune system

These medicines need regular blood tests to check they are not causing side effects, usually monthly at first and then less often [8]. Your hospital team and GP will usually share this care, following an agreed plan [8].

If you become seriously unwell with an infection while taking these medicines, contact your GP or specialist team, as some medicines may need to be paused until you recover [8].

## What is the outlook?

PUK can usually be controlled, but it often needs long-term treatment and follow-up, and stopping treatment completely is often not possible [6]. In a long-term study [9]:

- PUK came back in about {{risk.cornea.puk.recurrence}} eyes
- a hole formed in the cornea in about {{risk.cornea.puk.perforation}} eyes
- about {{risk.cornea.puk.moderate_vision_loss}} eyes had moderate loss of vision, and about {{risk.cornea.puk.severe_vision_loss}} had severe loss of vision

In a UK study, about {{risk.cornea.puk.transplant_uk}} people needed a corneal transplant after a hole formed, and most of these grafts stayed clear [2].

Please keep all your appointments with both your eye team and your rheumatology team, even if your eye feels better [6].

## When to get help urgently

Contact us straight away if your eye becomes more red or painful, more sensitive to light, or your vision gets worse [1, 10].
