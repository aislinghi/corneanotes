This leaflet is for people who have a herpes simplex infection of the eye, called herpes simplex keratitis. It explains what it is, how it is treated, why it can come back and what you can do. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is it?

Herpes simplex keratitis is an infection of the cornea, the clear window at the front of the eye, caused by the same virus that causes cold sores [1, 2]. Most people catch this virus in childhood. Up to {{risk.cornea.herpes_simplex.uk_carriers}} people in the UK carry it without knowing [3]. Once you have the virus it stays in the nerves for life, usually causing no problems, but it can become active again and cause a cold sore or an eye infection [2, 3]. It usually affects only one eye [1].

## What are the symptoms?

- eye pain
- a red, watery eye
- sensitivity to light
- blurred vision
- a swollen eyelid, or blisters or a rash around the eye [1]

## Types of infection

- **Surface infection.** The most common type affects the surface of the cornea, causing a sore with a branching shape called a dendritic ulcer [3].
- **Deeper inflammation.** The infection can also cause inflammation deeper in the cornea, which needs specialist treatment [4].
- Repeated episodes can scar the cornea and blur vision [3], and can damage the nerves of the cornea, so that it loses feeling [2].

## How is it treated?

- **Antiviral medicine,** as eye ointment, eye drops or sometimes tablets [1].
- **Steroid eye drops** are sometimes needed for deeper inflammation. These are only used under the care of an eye specialist, together with antiviral treatment [1, 4]. In a large trial, steroid drops given with antiviral drops helped deeper inflammation settle [5]. Do not stop or change steroid drops unless your eye team tells you to.

Most infections get better with treatment within a couple of weeks and do not permanently affect your eyesight [1].

## Will it come back?

It is common for it to come back [1]. In a large study, it came back within a year in about {{risk.cornea.herpes_simplex.recurrence_1y}} people, and within five years in about {{risk.cornea.herpes_simplex.recurrence_5y}} people [6].

People often wonder what triggers it. In a large trial, stress and other suspected triggers such as illness and eye injury were not shown to bring on attacks [7].

**Preventive tablets.** If it keeps coming back, your eye doctor may suggest taking antiviral tablets every day [1, 2]. In a large trial of people who had had an eye infection in the previous year, daily tablets for a year reduced the chance of it coming back from about {{risk.cornea.herpes_simplex.recurrence_placebo}} to about {{risk.cornea.herpes_simplex.recurrence_aciclovir}} people [8]. They were most useful for people who had had deeper inflammation of the cornea before [8].

## What is the outlook?

Most episodes heal well. Over many years, repeated episodes can lead to scarring, and some people need a corneal transplant [1, 3]. In a large study, about {{risk.cornea.herpes_simplex.serious_complication_20y}} people had a serious complication, such as severe loss of sight or needing surgery, within 20 years. Most of these people had had several episodes, and most were not taking preventive tablets [6].

## What can I do?

- **See an eye doctor as soon as possible each time you have symptoms,** so that treatment can start quickly [2].
- **Contact lenses:** do not wear them until 24 hours after all your symptoms have gone [1].
- **Cold sores:** the virus can spread to the eye from a cold sore, so avoid touching your eyes if you have one [2]. Wash your hands regularly [1].
- **Before eye treatment** such as cross-linking, tell your eye team that you have had herpes in the eye [9].

## When to get help urgently

Get an urgent appointment, or help from NHS 111, if you have eye pain, a red eye that is getting worse, or any change in your vision [1].

Go to A&E if you have a red eye and it hurts to look at light, the eye is very dark red, or one pupil is bigger than the other [1].
