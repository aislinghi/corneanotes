This leaflet is for people who have, or may have, Acanthamoeba keratitis. It explains what it is, how it is treated, what to expect, and how to avoid it in future. There is a separate leaflet on other types of eye infection (microbial keratitis). Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is Acanthamoeba keratitis?

Acanthamoeba keratitis is an infection of the cornea, the clear window at the front of the eye [1]. It is caused by Acanthamoeba, a tiny single-celled organism that is common in water, including tap water, swimming pools and hot tubs, and in soil [1, 2].

It is rare. Most cases are in contact lens wearers: in a UK national study, about {{risk.cornea.acanthamoeba.lens_wearer_share_uk}} people with it wore contact lenses, and about {{risk.cornea.acanthamoeba.yearly_risk_lens_wearers_uk}} contact lens wearers developed it each year [3]. Cases in the UK have increased since 2011 [3, 4].

It is extremely unlikely to spread from person to person [1].

## What makes it more likely?

The biggest risk is water getting onto contact lenses or lens cases [1, 4]. In UK studies:

- wearing lenses in a swimming pool or hot tub made it about {{risk.cornea.keratoconus_lenses.swimming_acanthamoeba}} more likely [4]
- not washing your hands before handling lenses, and poor lens care, also made it more likely [4]
- it was about {{risk.cornea.acanthamoeba.reusable_vs_daily_uk}} more common in people wearing reusable soft lenses (such as monthly lenses) than in people wearing daily disposable lenses [3]
- showering in lenses, wearing them overnight and reusing daily lenses all increased the risk in daily lens wearers [5]

Most cases could be avoided with good lens hygiene and keeping water away from lenses [4].

## What are the symptoms?

- pain, which can be severe, sometimes much worse than the eye looks [1, 6]
- redness and watering [2, 7]
- sensitivity to light [2]
- blurred vision [7]

In its early stages, Acanthamoeba keratitis can look like other eye infections, especially herpes simplex keratitis. In the UK study, about {{risk.cornea.acanthamoeba.misdiagnosed_herpes_uk}} cases were first thought to be herpes [3]. This can delay the right treatment [3, 8].

## Tests

Your eye team will examine your eye with a microscope. They may [1, 8]:

- take a small sample from the surface of the cornea (a scrape) to send to the laboratory
- take a swab to test for the organism's genetic material (a PCR test)
- use a special scanning microscope (confocal microscope) to look for the organism in the cornea

A negative test does not always rule out the infection [9]. **Bring your contact lenses, lens case and solutions with you,** as they can also be tested [8].

## How is it treated?

**Anti-amoebic eye drops** are the main treatment [1, 8]. Treatment is intensive at first:

- At the start, drops are usually needed every hour, often including through the night for the first few days. The drops are then reduced gradually [1, 9].
- You may need to stay in hospital for the first few days [2].
- Treatment usually lasts several months [1, 8]. In one study, the typical time to cure was about {{risk.cornea.acanthamoeba.treatment_months}}, but some people needed treatment for much longer [10].

The drops can sting. Keeping them in the fridge can help [1]. You may also be given painkillers, and drops to relax the eye and ease painful spasms [1].

**Steroid drops** reduce inflammation, but may also slow clearing of the infection [1]. Your specialist will decide whether you need them. Do not use steroid drops unless your eye specialist has prescribed them [11].

**A newer drop (polihexanide 0.08%)** was licensed in the UK in May 2025 [12]. In a European trial, about {{risk.cornea.acanthamoeba.cure_12m_trial}} people were cured with drops alone within a year, with either this drop or an older combination of drops [13]. NICE is assessing whether it should be used in the NHS in England [14]. Your eye team will tell you which drops are right for you.

## What is the outlook?

Most people are cured with drops, but the infection can be hard to treat [10, 13]. About a quarter of people are left with some permanent loss of vision [1].

- Some people need a corneal transplant, either to treat the infection or later to improve vision. In the recent European trial, about {{risk.cornea.acanthamoeba.transplant_trial}} people needed a transplant to treat the infection [13]. In earlier records from Moorfields Eye Hospital, a specialist referral centre, about {{risk.cornea.acanthamoeba.transplant_moorfields}} people had a transplant at some point [15].
- Less often, there can be other problems, such as inflammation of the white of the eye (scleritis), cataract or glaucoma [1, 16].

Being diagnosed and treated early gives the best chance of a good result [11].

## Contact lenses in future

- Do not wear contact lenses until your eye team says the infection has completely cleared [9]. This may take several weeks or months [1].
- If you go back to contact lenses, daily disposable lenses are usually the best choice [1].
- Follow the advice below to protect both eyes.

## Preventing Acanthamoeba keratitis

If you wear contact lenses [4, 9]:

- **never** let tap water touch your lenses or lens case
- **never** swim, shower or use a hot tub while wearing lenses
- wash and dry your hands thoroughly before handling lenses
- use fresh disinfecting solution every time, and keep your case clean
- consider daily disposable lenses, and never reuse them [3, 5]
- do not sleep in your lenses unless your contact lens practitioner has told you to [5]

## When to get help urgently

If you wear contact lenses and your eye becomes painful, red or sensitive to light, or your vision becomes blurred, **take your lenses out and get help the same day** [7, 8]. Keep your lenses, case and solutions [8].

If you are already being treated, contact us straight away if your pain, redness or blurred vision suddenly gets worse [9].
