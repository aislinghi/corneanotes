This leaflet is for people who have, or may have, an infection of the cornea, called microbial keratitis. It explains what it is, why it needs urgent treatment, how it is treated and how to lower the risk of it happening again. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is microbial keratitis?

Microbial keratitis is an infection of the cornea, the clear window at the front of your eye. It is usually caused by bacteria, and sometimes by fungi or by a tiny organism called acanthamoeba [1, 2, 9]. It can damage your sight, so it is treated as an emergency [1].

## What are the symptoms?

It usually starts suddenly, in one eye [1]:

- pain and redness
- watering, and sometimes a discharge
- light hurting the eye, making it hard to keep open
- blurred vision
- a feeling of something in the eye [2]

Sometimes you can see a small white spot on the cornea [2].

## What causes it?

The most common cause is wearing contact lenses [1]. Other causes include a scratch or injury to the eye, other eye surface problems, and previous eye surgery [1, 2].

Each year, about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.cl_incidence}} contact lens wearers develop microbial keratitis [3]. The risk is about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.overnight_risk}} higher if you sleep in your lenses [4]. In a UK study of people with infections linked to contact lenses, about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.cl_water}} had let their lenses come into contact with water, and about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.cl_overnight_share}} had slept in them [5].

## If you think you have an eye infection

- **Take your contact lenses out** and do not wear them again until you have been told it is safe [6].
- **Keep your lenses and lens case.** Do not throw them away. Bring them with you, as they may help to find the cause of the infection [1].
- **Get help the same day.** Contact us, your optician or NHS 111 if you have a painful red eye and wear contact lenses [1, 6]. Go to A&E if your sight has changed [6].

## Tests

After numbing your eye with drops, a small sample may be taken from the sore area on the cornea (a corneal scrape) [1, 2]. This helps to find which germ is causing the infection and which treatment will work best [2]. The results can take several days [2].

## Treatment

**Eye drops.** Treatment is with antibiotic eye drops (or other drops if the infection is caused by a fungus or acanthamoeba) [1]. At first, the drops are needed very often: up to every hour, and sometimes through the night [1, 2]. As the infection improves, you will need them less often [2]. Your drops may be changed if the infection is not getting better, or when the test results come back [2]. It is very important to use your drops exactly as prescribed.

**Staying in hospital.** Some people need to stay in hospital so that the drops can be given day and night [1]. In UK studies, about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.cl_hospital}} people with an infection linked to contact lenses [5], and about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.all_hospital}} people with bacterial keratitis of any cause [7], were admitted.

**Steroid drops.** Once the infection is under control, steroid drops are sometimes added [1]. Research has not shown clearly how much they help [8].

## What is the outlook?

Most infections clear with eye drops [5, 7], but the infection can leave a scar on the cornea that affects your vision, even with the right treatment [2].

**Infections linked to contact lenses.** In a UK study [5]:

- about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.cl_healed_drops}} people were cured with eye drops alone
- on average, the cornea took about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.cl_healing_time}} to heal
- about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.cl_vision_good}} people could see 6/12 or better at their last check-up
- about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.cl_transplant}} people later needed a corneal transplant because of scarring

**Bacterial keratitis of any cause.** In a UK study that included many people who already had other eye surface problems [7]:

- the cornea became very thin or developed a hole in about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.all_perforation}} people
- the infection came back in about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.all_recurrence}} people
- about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.all_eye_loss}} people lost the eye

## Acanthamoeba keratitis

Acanthamoeba is a tiny organism that can be found in water, including poorly treated tap water [9]. About {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.ak_cl_share}} cases of acanthamoeba keratitis happen in contact lens wearers [9]. Showering in lenses, and using reusable rather than daily disposable soft lenses, increase the risk [10]. Most cases could be prevented [11].

Treatment takes much longer than for other infections, with special drops given day and night at first, and often a stay in hospital [9]. Typically, it takes about {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.ak_cure_time}} to cure [12]. About {{risk.cornea.microbial_keratitis.ak_transplant}} people need a corneal transplant [13].

## Lowering the risk: looking after your contact lenses

Follow your contact lens practitioner's advice, and [14]:

- **never let water touch your lenses or lens case,** including tap water
- **do not sleep in your lenses** unless your practitioner has advised it
- **do not wear lenses for swimming, hot tubs or water sports, or in the shower**
- clean your lens case with solution, wipe it with a clean tissue and let it air-dry after each use
- replace your lens case at least every month
- never re-use or top up solution
- if your eyes do not feel good, look good or see well, take your lenses out and get advice straight away

## When to get help urgently

Contact us the same day if your eye becomes more painful or red, your vision gets worse, or you cannot use your drops as prescribed.
