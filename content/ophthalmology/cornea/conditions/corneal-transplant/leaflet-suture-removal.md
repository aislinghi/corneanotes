This leaflet is for people who have stitches in a corneal transplant, after a full-thickness graft (PK) or a deep anterior lamellar graft (DALK). It explains why and when the stitches are removed, what happens, and what to look out for afterwards. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## Why are the stitches there?

The very fine stitches hold your new cornea in place while it heals. They do not dissolve [1, 2]. You should not be able to see or feel them [3].

The stitches also affect the shape of the cornea, and so the way your eye focuses [1, 2].

## Why are they removed?

Once the cornea has healed, it is usually best to remove the stitches [1]. This:

- helps prevent problems later on, such as stitches breaking or becoming infected [1]
- lets the cornea settle into its final shape [1]

Sometimes a few stitches are removed or adjusted earlier, to reduce distortion of your vision (astigmatism) [2].

## When are they removed?

This is different for each person, and your surgeon will decide what is best for you. Stitches are usually removed gradually, a few at a time, at your follow-up appointments [3].

- **After a DALK,** the wound is stronger than after a full-thickness graft, so stitches can be removed sooner [2].

- **After a full-thickness graft (PK),** stitches are usually left in for longer [2].

{{site.aftercare.pk.stitches}}

## What happens?

Stitches are usually removed using numbing eye drops [1]. You may feel small tugs as each stitch comes out, but it should not be painful [1].

{{site.suture_removal.where}}

## Afterwards

- Your eye may feel scratchy, as if something is in it, for a short time. If it is sore, a painkiller such as ibuprofen may help [1].
- You will use eye drops, which usually include an antibiotic to prevent infection and a steroid to help prevent rejection [1]. Removing stitches is known to increase the risk of rejection, so please use the steroid drops exactly as advised [1, 5].
- The shape of your cornea, and so your glasses or contact lens prescription, will change as stitches are removed [1, 6]. After all the stitches are removed, the change can be hard to predict [6]. Your eye will usually be measured again a few weeks later [1].

## What are the risks?

Possible risks include:

- **Infection** [1]. In one study of full-thickness grafts, about {{risk.cornea.suture_removal.stitch_infection}} eyes developed an infection linked to the stitches at some point after the transplant [4].
- **Rejection** of the graft [1, 5]. See "When to get help" below.
- **The wound opening.** In the same study, the wound opened after stitches were removed in about {{risk.cornea.suture_removal.wound_opening}} grafts [4]. If this happens, new stitches may be needed, sometimes with another operation [1].

## Loose or broken stitches

Sometimes a stitch comes loose or breaks before it is due to be removed. This can make your eye feel as if there is grit in it. It may also be red, watery and sensitive to light, and your vision may be affected [7].

A loose or broken stitch in a transplant needs to be removed urgently, because it can lead to infection or rejection [8, 9]. In a UK study of infections after corneal transplants, a broken or loose stitch was a risk factor in about {{risk.cornea.suture_removal.loose_stitch_share_uk}} cases [9].

**If you think a stitch is loose or broken, contact us the same day** [8].

## When to get help

Contact us straight away if you have any of these symptoms. Remember **RSVP** [2, 3]:

- **R**edness
- **S**ensitivity to light
- **V**ision getting worse or more blurred
- **P**ain

These can be signs of rejection or infection. Rejection can usually be treated with steroids if it is found quickly [3].
