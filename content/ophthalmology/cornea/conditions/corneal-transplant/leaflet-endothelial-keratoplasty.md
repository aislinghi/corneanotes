This leaflet is for people who have been offered an endothelial keratoplasty, a transplant of the inner layer of the cornea. The two main types are called DMEK and DSAEK. It explains why the operation is needed, what it involves, how well it works, its risks and what to expect afterwards. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## The cornea and its inner layer

The cornea is the clear layer at the front of your eye. It lets light in so that you can see [1]. Its innermost layer, the endothelium, is a single layer of cells. It keeps the cornea clear by removing excess fluid from it [2]. If the endothelium stops working properly, the cornea swells and becomes cloudy, and your vision gets worse [2].

## Why does the inner layer stop working?

The most common reasons are [2]:

- **Fuchs endothelial dystrophy,** a condition that runs in families
- **damage to the inner layer,** for example after cataract surgery

Less often, it is caused by injury or infection [2].

**About Fuchs dystrophy.** Fuchs dystrophy is usually diagnosed in people aged 30 or over [3]. It is thought to affect about {{risk.cornea.fuchs.prevalence_over_40}} people over 40 in Europe and the United States, and women are affected up to three times more often than men [3]. It can be passed on from a parent to a child [3]. It causes glare, makes it harder to see contrast, and blurs vision [3]. Vision can be more blurred after waking, and salty (hypertonic saline) eye drops are sometimes used to help with this [3]. In advanced disease, the eye can become painful [3].

## What is an endothelial keratoplasty?

In an endothelial keratoplasty, only the damaged inner layer of your cornea is replaced with a healthy inner layer from a donor [2, 4]. The rest of your cornea stays in place [2]. The surgeon makes a small opening at the edge of the eye, removes the damaged inner layer, and places the donor layer against the back of your cornea [2]. A bubble of air inside the eye holds it in place while it sticks [4].

There are two main types, called DMEK and DSAEK [5]. Your surgeon will tell you which type is planned for you. They are now the most common corneal transplants in the UK: about {{risk.cornea.transplant.dmek_share}} corneal transplants are DMEK and about {{risk.cornea.transplant.dsaek_share}} are DSAEK [6].

If you also have a cataract, it may be removed in the same operation [7].

## What are the other options?

The other surgical option is a full-thickness corneal transplant (penetrating keratoplasty, or PK) [5]. Compared with a PK, vision usually recovers faster after an endothelial keratoplasty, although trials have not shown a clear difference in final vision [8]. You can also choose not to have surgery. Your eye specialist will talk through the options that suit you.

## The operation

- The operation usually takes 1 to 2 hours. Most people go home the same day [4].
- It can be done under local anaesthetic, so that you do not feel any pain, or under general anaesthetic, when you are asleep [2, 4].
- You will have a patch over your eye afterwards [4].

**Lying on your back.** Lying on your back helps the air bubble hold the new layer in place [4]. Units give different advice about how long to do this, and a recent review found that lying flat did not clearly change the chance of the graft coming loose [4, 9]. ## The donor cornea

Donor corneas come from people who chose to donate their corneas after they died [1]. The donor cornea is checked to make sure it is healthy before it is used [4].

Every tissue donor's health and medical history is carefully checked, and their blood is tested for infections that can be passed on through tissue, including HIV and hepatitis B and C [10, 11]. Any tissue that fails these tests is not used [10]. Donated tissue is also tested for bacteria and fungi [11]. There is no test for variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (vCJD). The risk of passing it on through a tissue transplant is believed to be very low, but it cannot be ruled out [10].

Corneas do not need to be matched to your tissue type [12].

## What are the risks?

**Rare but serious**

- **Infection inside the eye.** This happens after about {{risk.cornea.ek.endophthalmitis}} endothelial transplants [13].
- **Retinal detachment,** which needs further surgery [4].

**Less rare**

- **The new layer coming loose.** It may need another bubble of air. In a UK centre this was needed after about {{risk.cornea.ek.rebubble_dmek_uk}} DMEK operations, but across all published studies the average was about {{risk.cornea.ek.rebubble_dmek_high}} [14, 15]. After DSAEK, the graft came out of place in about {{risk.cornea.ek.detachment_dsek}} eyes on average [16].
- **Raised eye pressure.** This is one of the most common problems after DMEK [15]. Rarely, the air bubble blocks the flow of fluid inside the eye and the pressure rises suddenly. This happened in about {{risk.cornea.ek.pupil_block_uk}} eyes in a UK centre [17]. Sudden severe pain after the operation needs urgent attention.
- **The graft not clearing** (primary graft failure). This happened in about {{risk.cornea.ek.primary_failure_dmek}} DMEK operations and about {{risk.cornea.ek.primary_failure_dsek}} DSAEK operations on average [15, 16]. The operation can be repeated [2].
- **Rejection.** Your body's defences may attack the donor layer. In one US centre, rejection within two years happened in about {{risk.cornea.ek.rejection_2y_dmek}} people after DMEK and about {{risk.cornea.ek.rejection_2y_dsek}} people after DSAEK [18]. Rejection can happen at any time, and can usually be treated with steroids if it is found quickly [4].
- **Swelling at the back of the eye** (macular oedema), which can blur vision. Studies have found this in between about {{risk.cornea.ek.macular_swelling_low}} and about {{risk.cornea.ek.macular_swelling_high}} eyes after DMEK [19, 20]. In one study, all cases settled [19].
- **Cataract.** If you still have your own lens, about {{risk.cornea.ek.cataract_10y}} people need cataract surgery within ten years of DMEK [21].

## How well does it work?

- Vision usually improves within about six months [4]. Most people still need glasses [4].
- In a trial comparing the two types, about {{risk.cornea.ek.vision_6_7_5_dmek}} eyes could see 6/7.5 or better a year after DMEK, compared with about {{risk.cornea.ek.vision_6_7_5_dsek}} after DSAEK [22].
- In UK national data for first corneal transplants of all types, about {{risk.cornea.transplant.survival_5y_fed}} transplants for Fuchs dystrophy, and about {{risk.cornea.transplant.survival_5y_pbk}} transplants for clouding after cataract surgery, were still working after five years [6].
- In a large German study, about {{risk.cornea.ek.survival_10y_dmek}} DMEK grafts were still working after ten years [23].

## After the operation

- **Eye drops.** You will need to use eye drops for a long time to reduce the risk of the transplant failing [4]. {{site.aftercare.ek.drops}} Do not stop or change your drops unless your eye team tells you to.
- **Check-ups.** {{site.aftercare.ek.follow_up}}
- **Looking after your eye.** Do not rub your eye for the first few weeks. Be careful not to get water in your eye for at least a month [4].
- **Work.** {{site.aftercare.ek.time_off_work}} Ask your eye team what is right for your job.

## When to get help urgently

Contact us straight away if you have had a corneal transplant and you get [4]:

- a red eye
- eye pain
- sensitivity to light
- blurred vision
- floaters (dots and lines) that suddenly appear or increase
- flashes of light
- a dark "curtain" or shadow moving across your vision

These could be signs of rejection or a detached retina, which need treatment quickly [4].

## Information about your transplant

Your hospital is required to share information about your transplant with NHS Blood and Transplant, which supplies donor corneas, and you will be asked for your consent [24]. NHS Blood and Transplant keeps a register of corneal transplants, and clinicians should submit details of every endothelial transplant to it [2, 25]. If you do not consent, it may affect the supply of donor tissue for your transplant, or make it harder to contact you if a problem is later found with the tissue you received [24].
