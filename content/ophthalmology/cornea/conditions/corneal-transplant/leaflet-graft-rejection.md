This leaflet is for anyone who has had a corneal transplant (graft). It explains what rejection is, how to recognise it, what to do, and how it is treated. Please keep it somewhere safe, and ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is rejection?

Your body's immune system protects you by attacking things it does not recognise. After a corneal transplant, it can recognise the donor tissue as foreign and start to attack it. This is called rejection [1, 2].

Rejection most often affects the inner layer of the donor cornea, the endothelium [2, 3]. These cells keep the cornea clear, so if they are damaged, the graft can become cloudy and stop working [2].

**Rejection can usually be controlled if it is found early and treated quickly** [1, 2].

## When can it happen?

Rejection can happen at any time after a transplant, even many years later [1, 12]. The risk is highest in the first two years [5].

## How likely is it?

This depends on the type of transplant and the reason you needed it. For example:

- in UK data, about {{risk.cornea.pk.rejection_2y_kc}} adults who had a full-thickness graft (PK) for keratoconus had rejection within two years [6]
- in UK data, about {{risk.cornea.pk.rejection_2y_fed}} people who had a PK for Fuchs dystrophy had rejection within two years [7]
- after a DMEK graft (replacing only the inner layer), rejection is less common: about {{risk.cornea.ek.rejection_2y_dmek}} within two years in one US centre [8]
- after a DALK graft (keeping your own inner layer), rejection is less likely than after a PK [9]

Rejection is more likely if the eye was inflamed at the time of surgery, or if blood vessels have grown into the cornea [7, 10]. Grafts are also more likely to fail if you have had a transplant before [11].

## Symptoms: remember RSVP

Contact us straight away, the same day, if you notice any of these in an eye that has had a transplant [4, 12, 14]:

- **R**edness
- **S**ensitivity to light
- **V**ision getting worse or more blurred
- **P**ain or discomfort

If you cannot reach us, ask for an urgent GP appointment, call 111, or get help from 111 online [1].

**Rejection does not always cause symptoms.** In one study, about {{risk.cornea.graft_rejection.no_symptoms}} rejection episodes were found at routine check-ups in people who had not noticed anything wrong [13]. This is one reason why it is important to keep all your appointments.

## How is it treated?

Rejection is treated as an emergency [2, 14]. Treatment usually includes:

- **steroid eye drops used very often,** for example every hour, for a week or two, and then reduced slowly [3, 14]
- **steroid tablets or a drip** in more severe cases [14]
- sometimes other medicines that calm the immune system [14]
- antiviral medicine if you have had herpes simplex in the eye before [14]

Steroid drops can raise the pressure inside the eye, so your eye team will check it [15].

## What is the outlook?

Most rejection episodes can be reversed if they are treated early enough and strongly enough [16]. When a graft gets through a rejection episode, vision can return to how it was before [3].

However, rejection does not always respond to treatment:

- in a US study of grafts for keratoconus, about {{risk.cornea.graft_rejection.stayed_clear_us}} grafts that had a rejection episode stayed clear [13]
- in UK data for Fuchs dystrophy, about {{risk.cornea.graft_rejection.failed_after_rejection_uk}} grafts that had a recorded rejection episode later failed [7]

This is why acting quickly matters.

## Reducing the risk

- **Use your steroid drops exactly as prescribed.** Do not stop them unless your eye team tells you to [4, 5]. Some people need them for a long time, or for life [4].
- **Do not run out of drops.** Ask your GP for a repeat prescription in good time [5].
- **Keep all your appointments,** even if your eye feels fine [12, 13].
- **Vaccinations.** Your eye team may suggest using your steroid drops more often for a few days around a vaccination [5]. Large studies have not shown a clear increase in rejection after COVID-19 vaccination [17]. Ask your eye team for advice before a vaccination.

## If the graft fails

If a graft fails, it becomes cloudy and vision gets worse. Another transplant (a regraft) is often possible, but repeat grafts are less likely to last [5, 18]. In UK data, about {{risk.cornea.graft_rejection.regraft_survival_5y_uk}} second grafts were still working after five years [18].
