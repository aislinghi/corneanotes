This leaflet is for people who have been offered a penetrating keratoplasty, usually called PK. It explains what the operation involves, how well it works, its risks, and what to expect afterwards. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is the cornea?

The cornea is the clear layer at the front of your eye. It lets light in so that you can see [1]. It is made up of an inner, a middle and an outer layer [2].

## What is a PK?

A PK replaces the whole thickness of your cornea [2]. The surgeon removes a circle of your cornea about 1cm across and replaces it with a matching piece of donor cornea, held in place with very fine stitches [2].

About {{risk.cornea.transplant.pk_share}} corneal transplants in the UK are PKs [3]. A transplant can replace either all the layers of the cornea, or just the layers that are damaged [2]. Your surgeon will recommend the type that suits your eye.

## Why might I need one?

A transplant may be offered when glasses or contact lenses can no longer give you good enough vision, or to treat eye pain [2]. Reasons include [2, 4]:

- keratoconus, where the cornea becomes thin and cone-shaped
- clouding of the cornea, for example from Fuchs dystrophy or after cataract surgery
- damage from a severe eye infection or an injury
- a previous transplant that has failed

## What are the other options?

Depending on your eye, other options may include glasses or contact lenses, or a partial-thickness transplant [2]. You can also choose not to have surgery. Your eye specialist will talk through the options that suit you.

## The operation

- The operation takes 1 to 2 hours. Most people go home the same day, but some stay in hospital for a day or two [2].
- Most PKs in the UK are done under general anaesthetic, which means you are asleep [5]. During a PK the eye is open for a short time, which carries a small risk of serious bleeding inside the eye, and this is one reason a general anaesthetic is often preferred [5].
- You will have a patch over your eye afterwards [2].

## The donor cornea

Donor corneas come from people who chose to donate their corneas after they died [1]. The donor cornea is checked to make sure it is healthy before it is used [2].

Every tissue donor's health and medical history is carefully checked, and their blood is tested for infections that can be passed on through tissue, including HIV and hepatitis B and C [6, 7]. Any tissue that fails these tests is not used [6]. Donated tissue is also tested for bacteria and fungi [7].

There is no test for variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (vCJD). The risk of passing it on through a tissue transplant is believed to be very low, but it cannot be ruled out [6].

Unlike some other transplants, corneas do not need to be matched to your tissue type. There is no good evidence that matching helps [8, 9].

There are not enough donated corneas in the UK for everyone who needs one [1], so you may have to wait for your operation.

## What are the risks?

**Rare but serious**

- **Infection inside the eye.** In UK data, this happened after about {{risk.cornea.pk.endophthalmitis}} PKs, at any time after surgery [10]. It can seriously harm your sight.
- **Serious bleeding inside the eye** during the operation [5].
- **Retinal detachment,** which needs further surgery [2].

**Less rare**

- **Rejection.** Your body's defences may attack the donor cornea. In UK data, about {{risk.cornea.pk.rejection_2y_kc}} adults who had a PK for keratoconus [11], and about {{risk.cornea.pk.rejection_2y_fed}} people who had a PK for Fuchs dystrophy [12], had rejection within two years. Rejection can happen at any time, even years later, and can usually be treated with steroids if it is found quickly [2]. Rejection is more likely in younger people, and after later cataract surgery [9].
- **Raised eye pressure (glaucoma).** This affects about {{risk.cornea.pk.glaucoma}} people after a PK [13].
- **Infection on the surface of the eye,** which is treated with antibiotics [2].
- **Cataract** (clouding of the lens inside the eye), which can be removed with surgery [2].
- **The wound opening after an injury.** After a transplant, the eye always stays more easily damaged by an injury [14]. In one UK centre, about {{risk.cornea.pk.wound_injury}} eyes needed treatment after an injury opened the wound [14], so it is important to protect your eye from knocks [14].
- **Graft failure.** The cornea becomes cloudy again, and you may need more surgery [2].

## How well does it work?

How long a graft lasts depends on the reason for the transplant. In UK national data for first corneal transplants of all types [3]:

- about {{risk.cornea.transplant.survival_5y_kc}} transplants for keratoconus were still working after five years
- about {{risk.cornea.transplant.survival_5y_fed}} transplants for Fuchs dystrophy were still working after five years
- about {{risk.cornea.transplant.survival_5y_pbk}} transplants for clouding after cataract surgery were still working after five years

In a large US study of PKs for clouding of the cornea, about {{risk.cornea.pk.survival_10y_endothelial}} grafts were still working after ten years [15].

In UK data, about {{risk.cornea.pk.vision_6_6_kc}} people who had a PK for keratoconus could see 6/6 (very sharp vision) two years after surgery [16]. Most people still need glasses or contact lenses for their best vision afterwards [2]. To drive, you must be able to see at least 6/12 using both eyes together, with glasses or contact lenses if you need them [17]. It can take up to two years for your vision to improve fully [2].

## After the operation

- **Eye drops.** You will need to use eye drops for a long time to reduce the risk of the transplant failing [2]. {{site.aftercare.pk.drops}} Do not stop or change your drops unless your eye team tells you to.
- **Check-ups.** {{site.aftercare.pk.follow_up}}
- **Stitches.** {{site.aftercare.pk.stitches}}
- **Looking after your eye.** Do not rub your eye for the first few weeks. Be careful not to get water in your eye for at least a month [2].
- **Work.** {{site.aftercare.pk.time_off_work}} Ask your eye team what is right for your job.

## When to get help urgently

Contact us straight away if you have had a corneal transplant and you get [2]:

- a red eye
- eye pain
- sensitivity to light
- blurred vision
- floaters (dots and lines) that suddenly appear or increase
- flashes of light
- a dark "curtain" or shadow moving across your vision

These could be signs of rejection or a detached retina, which need treatment quickly [2].

## Information about your transplant

Your hospital is required to share information about your transplant with NHS Blood and Transplant, which supplies donor corneas, and you will be asked for your consent [18]. NHS Blood and Transplant keeps the UK Transplant Registry, a record of everyone who has received a transplant [19]. Information from the registry helps make transplants safer and more successful [19]. If you do not consent, it may affect the supply of donor tissue for your transplant, or make it harder to contact you if a problem is later found with the tissue you received [18].
