This leaflet is for people who have been offered a deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty, usually called DALK. It explains what the operation involves, how well it works, its risks, and what to expect afterwards. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is the cornea?

The cornea is the clear layer at the front of your eye. It lets light in so that you can see [1]. If it becomes thin, misshapen, scarred or cloudy, your vision can become blurred or distorted [1].

## What is a DALK?

A DALK is a corneal transplant in which only the front and middle layers of your cornea are replaced [2, 3]. The surgeon removes these layers and replaces them with the same layers from a donor cornea, held in place with very fine stitches [3]. Your own innermost layer, called the endothelium, is left in place [2].

## Why might I need one?

The most common reason is keratoconus, a condition in which the cornea becomes thinner and bulges into a cone shape [4]. Glasses, contact lenses and cross-linking treatment can help in mild to moderate keratoconus. A transplant may be needed when the condition is more severe [4]. About {{risk.cornea.keratoconus.transplant_needed}} people with keratoconus eventually need a corneal transplant [5].

DALK has become one of the main transplants used for keratoconus in the UK [6]. It can also be used for other conditions that affect the front layers of the cornea, as long as the inner layer is healthy [2].

## What are the other options?

Depending on your eye, other options may include glasses or contact lenses, or a full-thickness corneal transplant, called a penetrating keratoplasty or PK [4]. You can also choose not to have surgery. Your eye specialist will talk through the options that suit you.

## How is DALK different from a full-thickness transplant?

**Possible advantages of DALK**

- Because your own inner layer is kept, the type of rejection that attacks the inner layer cannot happen [2]. Rejection of the other layers can still happen (see "Risks" below).
- In clinical trials, rejection was less likely in the first year or two after DALK than after a full-thickness transplant [5]. Over many years, rejection rates were similar [7].
- In a long-term study, raised eye pressure needing treatment was less common after DALK than after a full-thickness transplant [7].
- The eye is not fully opened during a DALK, which lowers the risk of serious bleeding inside the eye during the operation [8].

**Possible disadvantages of DALK**

- In UK national data, people who had a DALK were slightly less likely to reach very sharp vision (6/6) two years after surgery than people who had a full-thickness transplant [9].
- In the same data, DALK grafts were more likely than full-thickness grafts to fail in the first month after surgery. After that, results were similar [9].

## Might my operation become a full-thickness transplant?

Sometimes the thin inner layer tears during the operation [10]. If this happens, the surgeon may need to finish the operation as a full-thickness transplant instead [10]. In UK hospital studies, this happened in about {{risk.cornea.dalk.conversion_manual}} operations done by hand, and about {{risk.cornea.dalk.conversion_laser}} operations done with the help of a laser [10, 11]. Changing to a full-thickness transplant raises the chance of rejection and of raised eye pressure later [10]. Your surgeon can tell you which technique they use.

## The operation

- Most DALK operations in the UK are done under general anaesthetic, which means you are asleep [8]. Some are done under local anaesthetic [3].
- The operation takes about an hour, and most people go home the same day [3].
- The part of your cornea that is removed is sent to the laboratory to be examined [3].

## The donor cornea

Donor corneas come from people who chose to donate their corneas after they died [1].

Every tissue donor's health and medical history is carefully checked, and their blood is tested for infections that can be passed on through tissue, including HIV and hepatitis B and C [12, 13]. Any tissue that fails these tests is not used [12]. Donated tissue is also tested for bacteria and fungi [13].

There is no test for variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (vCJD). The risk of passing it on through a tissue transplant is believed to be very low, but it cannot be ruled out [12].

Corneas do not need to be matched to your tissue type [19].

There are not enough donated corneas in the UK for everyone who needs one [1], so you may have to wait for your operation.

## What are the risks?

**Rare but serious**

- **Infection inside the eye.** This happens after about {{risk.cornea.dalk.endophthalmitis}} partial-thickness transplants [14]. It can seriously harm your sight.
- **Serious bleeding inside the eye** during the operation. This is less likely with DALK than with a full-thickness transplant [8].
- **Retinal detachment** [3].

**Less rare**

- **Rejection.** Your body's defences may attack the donor tissue. Studies have found that between about {{risk.cornea.dalk.rejection_lower}} and about {{risk.cornea.dalk.rejection_upper}} people have at least one episode of rejection after DALK. The higher figure comes from a study that followed people for about nine years [7, 15]. Rejection can happen months or years after surgery [15]. When it is treated promptly, the graft clears again in about {{risk.cornea.dalk.rejection_reversed}} cases [15].
- **Raised eye pressure (glaucoma).** About {{risk.cornea.dalk.raised_pressure}} people needed treatment for raised pressure in a long-term study [7]. It can usually be controlled with eye drops, but sometimes needs surgery and can harm your sight [3].
- **Infection of the graft** at some point after surgery. This affected about {{risk.cornea.dalk.infection_any}} people in a long-term study [7].
- **Cataract** (clouding of the lens inside the eye). This affected about {{risk.cornea.dalk.cataract}} people in a long-term study [7]. A cataract can be removed with surgery [3].
- **Graft failure.** The cornea becomes cloudy again and vision becomes blurred [3]. A failed graft can be replaced, but the risk of rejection and failure rises with each repeat transplant [3].

## How well does it work?

- About {{risk.cornea.dalk.graft_survival_5y}} DALK grafts for keratoconus are still clear five years after surgery [16].
- In a large UK study, about {{risk.cornea.dalk.vision_6_12_1y}} eyes could see 6/12 or better with glasses or contact lenses within a year of surgery. This rose to about {{risk.cornea.dalk.vision_6_12_after_sutures}} eyes once all the stitches had been removed [10].
- To drive, you must be able to see at least 6/12 using both eyes together (with glasses or contact lenses if you need them), and read a car number plate from 20 metres [17].
- Most people still need glasses or contact lenses for their best vision after a DALK, and some need further surgery [3].
- It can take up to 18 months for your vision to improve fully [3].

## After the operation

- **Check-ups.** {{site.aftercare.dalk.follow_up}}
- **Eye drops.** You will need to use anti-rejection eye drops for many months, and some people need them for life [3]. {{site.aftercare.dalk.drops}} In one study, rejection often happened after steroid drops were reduced or stopped, or after stitches were removed [15]. Do not stop or change your drops unless your eye team tells you to.
- **Stitches.** {{site.aftercare.dalk.stitches}}
- **Work.** {{site.aftercare.dalk.time_off_work}} Ask your eye team what is right for your job.

## Signs of rejection: get help the same day

Contact us straight away if you notice any of these in the eye that had the transplant [3]:

- redness
- sensitivity to light
- worse vision
- pain

Rejection needs urgent treatment, because delay can lead to the graft failing and loss of sight [3].

## Information about your transplant

Your hospital is required to share information about your transplant with NHS Blood and Transplant, which supplies donor corneas, and you will be asked for your consent [3]. NHS Blood and Transplant keeps the UK Transplant Registry, a record of everyone who has received a transplant [18]. Information from the registry helps make transplants safer and more successful [18]. If you do not consent, it may affect the supply of donor tissue for your transplant, or make it harder to contact you if a problem is later found with the tissue you received [3].
