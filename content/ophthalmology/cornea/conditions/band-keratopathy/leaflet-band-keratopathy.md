This leaflet is for people with band keratopathy. It explains what it is, why it happens, and what to expect from a treatment called EDTA chelation. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is band keratopathy?

Band keratopathy is a build-up of calcium in the front layers of the cornea, the clear window at the front of the eye [1]. It usually starts near the edges of the cornea and slowly spreads towards the centre over months or years, in a band across the eye [1].

## What are the symptoms?

In the early stages, most people have no symptoms [1, 2].

- **Blurred vision and glare,** if the calcium spreads across the centre of the cornea [2].
- **Discomfort.** The calcium can break through the surface of the cornea, causing irritation, a feeling of something in the eye, and sometimes painful episodes when the surface breaks down [1, 2].

## What causes it?

Band keratopathy can be linked to [1, 2]:

- long-term eye conditions, especially long-lasting inflammation inside the eye (uveitis)
- long-term use of eye drops, such as glaucoma drops. Some eye drops contain phosphate, which has been linked to calcium build-up [2]
- previous eye surgery, including surgery using silicone oil inside the eye [2]
- conditions that raise the level of calcium in the blood, or kidney failure

In a Scottish study, no cause was found in about {{risk.cornea.band_keratopathy.no_cause_found}} eyes. The most common known cause was long-term use of glaucoma drops, in about {{risk.cornea.band_keratopathy.glaucoma_drops}} eyes [2].

Children with long-lasting uveitis, including uveitis linked to juvenile idiopathic arthritis, can also develop band keratopathy [3].

Your eye team may ask about your eye drops, other medicines and supplements such as vitamin D and calcium, and may suggest blood or urine tests to look for a cause [1].

## Does it need treatment?

Not always. If it is not causing problems, it can simply be watched [1]. Mild discomfort can be helped by lubricating drops, gel or ointment, and sometimes a soft "bandage" contact lens [1].

Treatment to remove the calcium is usually offered if it is affecting your vision, or causing discomfort that other measures have not helped [1, 2]. If your vision is already poor because of another eye problem, the aim of treatment is to make the eye more comfortable, rather than to improve sight [2].

## What is EDTA chelation?

EDTA is a liquid that dissolves calcium. The procedure is usually done using numbing eye drops [1, 2].

1. The surface layer of cells on the cornea is removed. Sometimes a weak alcohol solution is used to loosen it [2].
2. EDTA is placed on the cornea for a few minutes at a time, and the loosened calcium is gently scraped away. This is repeated until enough calcium has been removed [1, 2].
3. A soft "bandage" contact lens is put on the eye to protect it while the surface heals [1, 2].

In a Scottish study, the procedure took about {{risk.cornea.band_keratopathy.operation_time}} on average [2].

## What are the benefits?

In a Scottish study of 89 eyes treated by five surgeons in one NHS health board [2]:

- the centre of the cornea was clear afterwards in about {{risk.cornea.band_keratopathy.clear_centre}} eyes
- vision stayed the same or improved in about {{risk.cornea.band_keratopathy.vision_same_or_better}} eyes, but it improved clearly (by two lines or more on the sight chart) in only about {{risk.cornea.band_keratopathy.vision_clearly_better}} eyes
- among people with very poor vision, about {{risk.cornea.band_keratopathy.comfort_poor_vision}} said their eye was more comfortable

How much your vision improves depends on how much the calcium is affecting it, and whether you have other eye problems [1, 2].

## What are the risks?

- **Calcium can come back,** because the treatment removes the calcium but not its underlying cause [2]. In the Scottish study, some calcium came back in about {{risk.cornea.band_keratopathy.calcium_returns}} eyes, but only about {{risk.cornea.band_keratopathy.repeat_treatment}} eyes needed the treatment again [2]. It can be repeated if needed.
- **Slow healing.** In the same study there were no recorded complications from surgery, but in about {{risk.cornea.band_keratopathy.slow_healing}} eyes the surface had not fully healed after four weeks [2].
- **Infection.** While the surface is healing, the eye is more open to infection [4]. See "When to get help" below.
- **Thinning or melting of the cornea** has been reported rarely [5, 6]. The risk appears to be higher in eyes that have had shingles (herpes zoster) affecting the eye: in a New Zealand study, {{risk.cornea.band_keratopathy.zoster_melt}} people with shingles affecting the eye who had EDTA chelation developed melting of the cornea [5]. If you have had shingles affecting your eye, your surgeon will discuss this with you.
- **An uneven surface.** Removing the calcium too firmly can leave the surface uneven, which can affect vision [1].

## Other treatments

- **PTK laser treatment** (phototherapeutic keratectomy) uses a laser to remove the calcium and smooth the surface [7, 8]. NICE has found the evidence good enough for it to be used routinely [7]. In a German study, vision and comfort improved as much after PTK as after EDTA, and the calcium came back less often after PTK [9].
- **Amniotic membrane,** a thin natural tissue placed on the eye to help it heal, is sometimes used as well, in selected cases [8]. There is a separate leaflet on amniotic membrane.
