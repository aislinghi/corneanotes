This leaflet is for people who have been told they have an inherited corneal dystrophy, such as granular, lattice, Reis-Bücklers or macular dystrophy. It explains what these conditions are, how they are passed on in families, how they are treated, and why it matters if you are thinking about laser eye surgery. Fuchs dystrophy and EBMD have their own leaflets. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is a corneal dystrophy?

The cornea is the clear window at the front of the eye. In a corneal dystrophy, abnormal material slowly builds up in one or more of its layers, making parts of it cloudy [1, 2]. There are more than 20 types [2].

Corneal dystrophies are usually inherited, usually affect both eyes, and usually get worse only slowly [1, 2]. They are not caused by infection, or by anything in your surroundings [1].

Many people have no problems for years, and some never need treatment [1]. Some people see surprisingly well even when the cornea looks cloudy to the eye specialist [1].

## The main types

Your eye team will tell you which type you have. The main types covered by this leaflet are:

- **Granular dystrophy (type 1).** Glare and light sensitivity are early symptoms. Vision slowly gets worse with age as the cloudy spots join up. Painful erosions (see below) can happen [1].
- **Granular dystrophy (type 2),** sometimes called Avellino dystrophy. This usually causes fewer erosions and less cloudiness than type 1 [1]. **It can get much worse after any injury to the cornea, including laser eye surgery** (see below) [1, 3].
- **Lattice dystrophy.** Painful erosions and discomfort can start in childhood or the teenage years, and vision is often affected by the age of 40 [1].
- **Reis-Bücklers and Thiel-Behnke dystrophies.** These affect the front layers of the cornea. They often cause painful erosions from childhood, which tend to become less frequent with time, while vision slowly gets worse [1].
- **Macular dystrophy.** This can affect vision more severely, often between the ages of 10 and 30 [1].

## Erosions: painful attacks

In several dystrophies, the surface layer of the cornea can break down, causing painful attacks called erosions [1]. The treatment is the same as for other types of recurrent erosion, mainly lubricating drops and ointment [1]. There is a separate leaflet on recurrent corneal erosion.

## How are dystrophies passed on?

- **Granular, lattice, Reis-Bücklers and Thiel-Behnke dystrophies** are caused by changes in a gene called TGFBI, and are passed on in a "dominant" way [1]. If one parent has the condition, each child has a 1 in 2 chance of inheriting it [2].
- **Macular dystrophy** is passed on in a "recessive" way. A child is affected only if they inherit a changed gene from both parents. When both parents carry the gene, each child has a 1 in 4 chance of having the condition, and a 1 in 2 chance of being a carrier [1, 2].

Within a family, the condition can be mild in one person and more severe in another. Occasionally, someone carries the gene change but shows no signs [1]. A dystrophy can also appear for the first time in a family [1].

**Genetic testing.** Genetic testing can confirm which dystrophy you have [1]. In England, the NHS Genomic Medicine Service offers a test for inherited corneal dystrophies (test R262), which includes the TGFBI gene [4]. Your eye team can tell you whether you, or your family, might be offered testing or genetic counselling [2].

## Treatment

- **No treatment** is needed if the dystrophy is not causing problems [1].
- **Lubricating drops and ointment, and sometimes a bandage contact lens,** for erosions and discomfort [1].
- **PTK laser treatment** (phototherapeutic keratectomy). A laser removes cloudy material from the front layers of the cornea [1]. In a German study of granular and lattice dystrophy, vision improved in most eyes after PTK [5]. The dystrophy can come back, but PTK can be repeated [1, 6]. In the same study, the dystrophy came back in about {{risk.cornea.dystrophies.ptk_recurrence}} eyes over about three years [5].
- **A corneal transplant,** if the cloudiness is deeper or PTK is not enough. This may replace the front layers only (DALK) or the full thickness of the cornea (PK) [1]. There are separate leaflets on these operations.

**The dystrophy can come back in a transplant,** because the abnormal material comes from your own eye [1]. It usually comes back later after a transplant than after PTK [1]. In a US study of lattice dystrophy, it came back enough to cause erosions or affect vision in about {{risk.cornea.dystrophies.lattice_graft_recurrence_5y}} transplants within five years [7]. Macular dystrophy comes back in a transplant less often than granular or lattice dystrophy [1, 7].

## Laser eye surgery: important

Laser eye surgery to correct short or long sight (such as LASIK, PRK, LASEK or SMILE) can make some dystrophies much worse. In granular dystrophy type 2, it is strongly advised against, because it can cause the cloudy deposits to build up quickly [1, 3].

**If you have a corneal dystrophy, or one runs in your family, tell any eye surgeon or optometrist before you have laser eye surgery or any other operation on your eyes.**
