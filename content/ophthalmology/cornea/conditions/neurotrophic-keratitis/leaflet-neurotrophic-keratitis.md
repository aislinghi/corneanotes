This leaflet is for people with neurotrophic keratitis, sometimes called neurotrophic keratopathy. It explains what it is, why it needs careful follow-up even when your eye does not hurt, and the treatments available. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is neurotrophic keratitis?

The cornea is the clear window at the front of the eye. It has many nerves, which give it feeling. These nerves also keep the cornea healthy: they trigger blinking, help you make tears, and help the surface heal [1, 2].

In neurotrophic keratitis, these nerves are damaged, so the cornea loses some or all of its feeling [1, 3]. You blink less, and the surface of the cornea does not heal properly [2, 3]. This can lead to [1, 3, 4]:

- patches where the surface layer of the cornea breaks down and does not heal
- an ulcer, a deeper sore in the cornea
- in severe cases, thinning of the cornea, or a hole forming in it

The cornea is also more likely to become infected [1, 2].

Neurotrophic keratitis is rare [3, 4].

## What causes it?

The nerves to the cornea can be damaged by [3, 4, 5]:

- **herpes infections of the eye,** both herpes simplex (cold sore virus) and herpes zoster (shingles). These are among the most common causes
- **surgery or a growth affecting the nerve inside the head,** for example operations for trigeminal neuralgia or brain tumours
- **eye injuries,** including chemical injuries
- **eye surgery,** including corneal transplants and laser eye surgery
- **long-term use of eye drops**
- **long-term contact lens wear**
- **conditions such as diabetes and multiple sclerosis**

There are separate leaflets on herpes simplex keratitis and herpes zoster ophthalmicus.

## What are the symptoms?

Because the cornea has lost feeling, many people have few symptoms, even when the cornea is damaged [4, 5]. Some people do have pain [3]. You may notice [3, 5]:

- redness
- blurred vision
- watering or sensitivity to light

**This is why regular check-ups are so important.** Your eye can get worse without you feeling it, so your eye team needs to look at it [4, 5].

## What can I do?

- **Use your drops exactly as prescribed,** on a regular schedule, not just when your eye feels uncomfortable. You may not be able to feel when your eye needs them [5].
- **Use preservative-free drops.** Preservatives in eye drops can damage the surface of the eye, so your eye team may stop drops you do not need, or change them to preservative-free ones [4]. Do not stop any drops without checking with us first.
- **Keep all your appointments,** even if your eye feels fine [4, 5].

## Treatments

There is no single best treatment, and research has not yet shown clearly which treatments work best [3]. Your treatment will depend on how severe your condition is [3, 4]:

- **Preservative-free lubricating drops, gel or ointment** to protect and support the surface of the eye [1, 3, 4].
- **Antibiotic drops** to help prevent infection while the surface is broken down [1, 4].
- **A soft "bandage" contact lens** to protect the surface while it heals [3, 4]. Contact lenses may increase the risk of infection, so your eye team will weigh this up [4].
- **Plugs in the tear ducts** to keep tears on the eye for longer [4, 5].
- **Serum eye drops,** made from blood, which may help the surface heal [3, 5].
- **Closing the eyelid** to protect the cornea while it heals. This can be done with an injection of botulinum toxin, which makes the upper eyelid droop for a few months [5], or with stitches that join part of the eyelids together (tarsorrhaphy) [1, 3, 4].
- **Amniotic membrane,** a thin natural tissue placed on the eye to help it heal [1, 3]. There is a separate leaflet on amniotic membrane.

Steroid eye drops need to be used with great care in neurotrophic keratitis, because they can slow healing and make thinning of the cornea more likely [4, 5].

**Nerve growth factor drops (cenegermin).** You may read about these drops online. They are no longer available in the UK [6].

**Nerve graft surgery (corneal neurotisation).** In this operation, a nerve is taken from elsewhere and joined to a healthy nerve near the eye, so that new nerve endings grow into the cornea over several months [2]. The aim is to protect the cornea by helping it heal [2]. NICE has said that the evidence is still limited, so it is only done in specialist centres, with special arrangements for consent and audit or research [7].

## What is the outlook?

The outlook depends on what damaged the nerve and how badly, and on whether you have other problems with the surface of the eye [4]. You will need regular check-ups, because the condition can get worse without symptoms [4, 5].

## When to get help urgently

Remember that your eye may not hurt even when something is wrong. Contact us straight away if:

- your eye becomes more red [8]
- your vision becomes more blurred or changes [8]
- it hurts to look at light [8]
- you wear a bandage contact lens and your eye becomes red [8]
