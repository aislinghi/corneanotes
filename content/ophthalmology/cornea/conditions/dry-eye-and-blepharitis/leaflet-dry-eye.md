This leaflet is for people with dry eye. It explains what dry eye is, what you can do to help, and the treatments available. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is dry eye?

Dry eye happens when your eyes do not make enough tears, or your tears dry up too quickly [1, 2]. It is common: a UK study found that about {{risk.cornea.dry_eye.uk_prevalence}} adults have it [3]. It becomes more common with age, and is more common in women [2, 4].

## What are the symptoms?

Your eyes may feel [1]:

- gritty, sore or itchy
- red
- blurry
- sensitive to light
- more watery than normal

Symptoms are often worse in windy or dry conditions, or in smoky places [4].

## What makes it more likely?

You may be more likely to have dry eyes if [1]:

- you are over 50
- you wear contact lenses
- you look at screens for a long time without a break
- you spend time in air-conditioned or heated rooms, or windy, dry or dusty places
- you smoke or drink alcohol
- you take certain medicines, such as some antidepressants or blood pressure medicines [1], or antihistamines [2]
- you have a condition such as blepharitis, Sjögren's syndrome or lupus

It is also more common in women after the menopause [4].

## What can I do?

- **Clean your eyelids every day** [1]. There is a separate leaflet on blepharitis, which explains how.
- **Take breaks from screens,** and remember to blink [1, 4].
- **Keep your screen just below eye level** [1].
- **Use a humidifier** to stop the air getting dry [1].
- **Avoid smoky, dry or dusty places,** and long periods in air-conditioned or heated rooms [1].
- **Do not smoke,** and do not drink too much alcohol [1].
- **If you wear contact lenses,** take them out and wear glasses to rest your eyes [1].
- **Do not stop a prescribed medicine** without talking to your doctor first [1].

## Lubricating eye drops (artificial tears)

Lubricating drops are the main treatment for dry eye [5], and can be bought from pharmacies. Research suggests they are generally safe and can help, although the evidence is of low quality [6].

- If you need drops **more than six times a day,** or you **wear contact lenses,** use **preservative-free** drops [7].
- **Eye ointment** can help too, but it blurs vision, so it is usually best used at night [7].

## Further treatments

If your dry eye is severe and does not get better with lubricants, other treatments may be considered:

- **Ciclosporin eye drops,** which reduce inflammation. NICE recommends them for adults with dry eye that has caused severe inflammation of the cornea (keratitis) and has not improved with lubricants [8]. They may improve symptoms, but people using them are more likely to stop because of side effects [9].
- **Punctal plugs,** tiny plugs placed in the tear ducts to keep tears on the eye for longer [4]. Research has not shown clearly that they improve symptoms, and they can make the eyes water too much [10].
- **Serum eye drops,** made from your own blood. They may help in the short term, but the evidence is limited [11].
- **Omega-3 (fish oil) supplements** did not help more than a dummy pill in a large trial [12], and the evidence overall is uncertain [13].
- **Intense pulsed light (IPL)**, a light treatment for the eyelid oil glands, has been studied, but it is uncertain whether it helps [14].

## Is it serious?

In some people, dry eye can damage the surface of the eye [4].

## Could it be a sign of something else?

Dry eyes can be part of Sjögren's syndrome, a condition in which the immune system affects the glands that make tears and saliva. Other symptoms can include a dry mouth, aching muscles and joints, and extreme tiredness [15]. See your GP if you have these symptoms and they are affecting your daily life [15].

## When to get help

Get an urgent GP appointment or help from NHS 111 if your eye is painful and red, or you have a red eye and wear contact lenses [1].

Go to A&E or call 999 if you have changes to your sight, it hurts to look at light, or your eye is very dark red [1].
