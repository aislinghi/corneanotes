This leaflet is for people who have keratoconus, or who are being checked for it, and their families. It explains what keratoconus is, how it is monitored and how it can be treated. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is keratoconus?

The cornea is the clear window at the front of your eye. In keratoconus, the cornea becomes thinner and starts to bulge forward into a cone shape [1, 2]. This changes how the eye focuses light, causing short sight and astigmatism, so that vision becomes blurred or distorted [1, 2].

Keratoconus does not cause blindness. Even when it is advanced, vision can usually be corrected with contact lenses [2].

## Who gets it?

Worldwide, about {{risk.cornea.keratoconus.prevalence_world}} people have keratoconus, but this varies a great deal between parts of the world. It is much more common in South Asia [3]. In a UK study, keratoconus was diagnosed about {{risk.cornea.keratoconus.incidence_asian_vs_white_uk}} as often in people of Asian origin as in white people [4].

Keratoconus usually shows itself around puberty, in the late teens or in the early twenties [5].

## What causes it?

The exact cause is not known. Both genes and the environment seem to play a part [2]. Keratoconus is more likely in people who:

- rub their eyes [6]
- have a close relative with keratoconus [6]
- have allergies such as eczema, asthma or hay fever [6]

About {{risk.cornea.keratoconus.family_affected}} people with keratoconus have a parent, brother, sister or child who also has it [2]. If you have keratoconus, it may be worth your close family having their eyes checked [7].

## Will it get worse?

Keratoconus can get worse over time [1]. It is more likely to get worse in younger people and in people whose cornea is already steeper [8]. As you get older, the cornea naturally becomes stiffer, and keratoconus usually stops getting worse by your mid to late thirties [5].

## Why do my eyes need to be checked regularly?

Regular scans of the shape and thickness of your cornea show whether your keratoconus is changing [5]. If it is getting worse, you may be offered a treatment called cross-linking to stop it getting worse (see below) [9].

## What can I do to help?

- **Try not to rub your eyes.** Eye rubbing is linked to keratoconus getting worse [7]. In one study, most people whose keratoconus kept getting worse had carried on rubbing their eyes [10].
- **Get treatment for allergies,** such as itchy eyes, as these are linked to keratoconus getting worse [7].

## How is keratoconus treated?

Treatment depends on how much your cornea has changed:

- **Glasses or soft contact lenses** can correct vision in the early stages [1, 5].
- **Rigid gas permeable (hard) contact lenses** are often needed as the cornea becomes steeper and more irregular [5]. Contact lenses do not make keratoconus better or worse. They improve your vision while you wear them [11].
- **Scleral lenses,** a special type of rigid lens, can improve vision and comfort for many people with keratoconus [12].
- **Corneal implants** (intracorneal ring segments) are used for some people [1].
- **Cross-linking** uses vitamin B2 (riboflavin) eye drops and ultraviolet light to strengthen the cornea and stop keratoconus getting worse [9, 13]. Its aim is to keep your vision stable, not to improve it, so you will still need glasses or contact lenses afterwards [9].
- **A corneal transplant** may be needed if contact lenses can no longer give good vision [1, 5]. About {{risk.cornea.keratoconus.transplant_needed}} people with keratoconus eventually need a transplant [14]. Fewer transplants have been needed since cross-linking became available [5, 15].

## When to get help urgently

A small number of people with keratoconus have a sudden, painful loss of vision. This is caused by fluid getting into a very thin part of the cornea (called hydrops), and it usually settles with time [2]. If this happens, contact us the same day.
