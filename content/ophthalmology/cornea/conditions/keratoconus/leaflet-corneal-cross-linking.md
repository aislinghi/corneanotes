This leaflet is for people with keratoconus who have been offered corneal cross-linking, and their families. It explains what the treatment involves, how well it works, its risks and what to expect afterwards. There is a separate leaflet about keratoconus itself. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is cross-linking?

Cross-linking is a treatment to strengthen the cornea, the clear window at the front of the eye, so that keratoconus stops getting worse [1, 2]. It uses vitamin B2 (riboflavin) eye drops and ultraviolet light [1, 2].

The aim is to keep your vision stable, not to improve it. You will still need glasses or contact lenses afterwards [2].

## Who is it for?

Cross-linking is usually offered when scans show that your keratoconus is getting worse [2]. Before offering it, your eye team will check how thick your cornea is and how likely your keratoconus is to keep changing [3].

In the UK, NICE (the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence) has found that the evidence for the standard ("epithelium-off") method is good enough for it to be used routinely [3]. Other methods, such as leaving the surface layer in place ("epithelium-on"), should only be used in research or with extra safeguards [3].

## How well does it work?

- In a UK trial of 10 to 16 year olds, keratoconus got worse within 18 months in about {{risk.cornea.cxl.keralink_progression_treated}} people who had cross-linking, compared with about {{risk.cornea.cxl.keralink_progression_untreated}} people who did not [4].
- In a study of mostly adults, keratoconus still got worse in the year after treatment in about {{risk.cornea.cxl.progression_1y_adults}} eyes [5].
- The long-term benefit is less certain [1]. In one long-term study, the cornea continued to steepen in about {{risk.cornea.cxl.nonresponse_10y}} eyes ten years after treatment [6].
- Some people need a second treatment. In children and young people, about {{risk.cornea.cxl.retreatment_10y}} needed treatment again within ten years [7].

## The treatment

{{site.cxl.where}}

- It is usually done as a day case, using numbing eye drops rather than a general anaesthetic [1, 2].
- The thin surface layer of the cornea is gently removed, so that the riboflavin drops can soak in [1, 2].
- Riboflavin drops are put on the eye, and then ultraviolet light is shone onto the cornea [1, 2].
- A soft "bandage" contact lens is put on the eye at the end to help it heal [1, 2].

{{site.cxl.technique}} {{site.cxl.both_eyes}}

If you have ever had cold sores in or around your eye (herpes simplex), please tell your eye team before the treatment [8].

## After the treatment

**Pain.** Your eye is likely to be sore, gritty, red and sensitive to light for several days [2]. Some people have very little discomfort, but for others the pain can be severe, especially in the first few days [2, 8, 9]. {{site.aftercare.cxl.pain_relief}}

**The bandage contact lens** usually stays in for about a week, until the surface of the eye has healed. If it falls out, do not put it back in [2].

**Your vision** will be hazy and blurred at first, and should clear over the first few weeks [2]. A faint haze in the cornea is common. It is usually at its most noticeable about a month after treatment, and usually fades within three to six months [7, 10]. Most people's vision returns to the same level as before treatment [2].

## What are the risks?

Serious problems are uncommon: fewer than about {{risk.cornea.cxl.severe_adverse}} people have a severe complication [11].

- **Infection of the cornea.** This happens after about {{risk.cornea.cxl.infection}} treatments [12]. It can harm your sight, so it needs urgent treatment (see below).
- **Loss of some sharpness of vision.** About {{risk.cornea.cxl.vision_loss}} eyes lost two or more lines on the sight chart a year after treatment. This was more likely in people over 35 and in people whose vision was already good before treatment [5].
- **Scarring** in the centre of the cornea, which can affect vision. This happened in about {{risk.cornea.cxl.scarring}} eyes in one study [5].
- **Inflammation spots** in the cornea that are not caused by infection. These happened in about {{risk.cornea.cxl.sterile_infiltrates}} eyes in the same study [5].
- **The treatment not working,** so that keratoconus keeps getting worse (see "How well does it work?" above).

In a large US trial, the number of cells in the inner layer of the cornea (the endothelium) did not change after cross-linking [13].

## When to get help urgently

Pain that comes and goes in the first two days is normal. If your pain gets worse rather than better three or four days after treatment, or your eye becomes more red, this could be a sign of infection [2, 8]. Contact us the same day.
