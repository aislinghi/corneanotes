This leaflet is for people with keratoconus who wear, or are about to start wearing, contact lenses. It explains why lenses help, the different types, what fitting involves, and how to wear them safely. There is a separate leaflet about keratoconus itself. Please ask your contact lens team about anything that is not clear.

## Why do I need contact lenses?

In keratoconus, the cornea (the clear window at the front of the eye) becomes thin and cone-shaped, and its surface becomes uneven. In the early stages, glasses can give good vision. As the cornea becomes more irregular, glasses may no longer work well [1, 2].

Contact lenses, especially rigid lenses, give the eye a new, smooth front surface. A thin layer of tears fills the gap between the lens and the uneven cornea, which corrects much of the distortion [1, 2, 3]. Contact lenses are the main way of correcting vision for most people with keratoconus [3]. In a Scottish study, about {{risk.cornea.keratoconus_lenses.rigid_share_uk}} people with keratoconus wore rigid lenses [4].

Contact lenses improve your vision while you wear them. They do not cure keratoconus or stop it changing [5].

## Types of lens

No single type suits everyone [2].

- **Rigid gas permeable (RGP) lenses** are the most common choice. They usually give the best vision, and are fairly easy to put in, take out and look after [1, 3].
- **Soft lenses,** including thicker soft lenses made for keratoconus, are often more comfortable but may not give such good vision, especially in more advanced keratoconus [1, 2, 3].
- **Hybrid lenses** have a rigid centre for clear vision and a soft outer edge for comfort. They can help people who cannot get on with rigid lenses [2, 3].
- **Scleral and mini-scleral lenses** are large rigid lenses that rest on the white of the eye and arch over the cornea. They are filled with sterile saline before you put them in [2]. They tend to be more comfortable than smaller rigid lenses, with similar vision [3, 6]. They are often used in more advanced keratoconus [1, 2].
- **"Piggyback" lenses** are a soft lens with a rigid lens on top. They are mainly used for a short time [2, 3].

In a UK trial comparing smaller rigid lenses with scleral lenses, people found scleral lenses more comfortable, but about half preferred each type overall [6].

## Fitting your lenses

- Your first fitting appointment can take a long time, because several trial lenses may be tried [1, 7].
- In keratoconus, the first lens is often not quite right, and several fittings and adjustments may be needed [1, 2].
- Rigid lenses often feel a little uncomfortable at first, but this usually gets better with time. A small number of people cannot get used to them [1]. Build up your wearing time as your contact lens team advises [8].
- The shape of your cornea can change over time, so your lenses may need to be refitted [1].
- You may still need glasses over your lenses for some tasks, such as reading [8].

## Caring for your lenses

Contact lens infections can be serious and can damage your sight [10, 12]. Following these steps lowers the risk.

- **Wash your hands** before touching your lenses [1, 11].
- **Keep water away from your lenses.** Never rinse or store lenses or cases in tap water [10, 12]. Do not wear lenses for swimming, in a hot tub, or in the shower [1, 11]. In a UK study, wearing lenses in a swimming pool or hot tub made a serious infection called Acanthamoeba keratitis about {{risk.cornea.keratoconus_lenses.swimming_acanthamoeba}} more likely [11].
- **Do not sleep in your lenses** unless your contact lens team has told you to. In a UK study, wearing lenses overnight made infections about {{risk.cornea.keratoconus_lenses.overnight_infection}} more likely [9].
- **Clean and store your lenses** exactly as your contact lens team shows you, using the solutions they recommend [1].
- **Use fresh solution every day, and keep your case clean** [1].
- **For scleral lenses,** fill the lens with sterile, preservative-free saline, never with tap water [2, 3].

## Contact lenses and scans

Contact lenses change the shape of the cornea for a while, so you may be asked to leave them out before your scans. There is more about this in the keratoconus leaflet.

## Lenses after cross-linking or a transplant

- **After cross-linking,** your eye team will tell you when you can go back to your lenses.
- **After a corneal transplant,** many people still need contact lenses to see well. Studies suggest about {{risk.cornea.keratoconus_lenses.lenses_after_pk}} people with keratoconus go back to contact lenses after a full-thickness transplant [3].
- **After corneal hydrops** (a sudden swelling of the cornea), do not wear lenses until your eye team says so. Your lenses may need to be refitted, because the shape of the cornea can change [13].

## Driving

If you need your lenses to meet the eyesight standard for driving, you must wear them every time you drive [14]. You must tell the DVLA if you have an eyesight problem affecting both eyes [14].

## Eye rubbing

Rubbing your eyes is linked to keratoconus [15]. Try not to rub your eyes, with or without your lenses in.

## When to get help urgently

**If your eye becomes red or painful, or your vision gets worse, take your lens out and get help straight away** [1, 10]. An infection is an emergency, so do not wait to see if it settles [10].

Keep your lenses and case and bring them with you, as they can help find the cause of an infection [10].
