This leaflet is for people with Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy, usually called Fuchs dystrophy. It explains what it is, what you can do, why it matters if you need cataract surgery, and the treatments available. Please ask your eye team about anything that is not clear.

## What is Fuchs dystrophy?

The cornea is the clear window at the front of the eye. Its inner surface is lined by a layer of cells called the endothelium. These cells pump fluid out of the cornea to keep it clear [1, 2].

Everyone loses some of these cells as they get older. In Fuchs dystrophy, they are lost faster than normal and stop working properly. Fluid builds up, so the cornea swells and becomes cloudy, and vision becomes hazy [1, 2].

Fuchs dystrophy affects both eyes, and usually gets worse slowly [1]. It is not an infection, and there is no inflammation [1].

## Who gets it?

- It is thought to affect about {{risk.cornea.fuchs.prevalence_over_40}} people over 40 in Europe and the United States [5].
- Signs can be seen in people in their 30s and 40s, but symptoms do not usually start until people are in their 50s or older [2].
- It is more common in women than in men [1, 2].
- It often runs in families, but it can also happen in people with no family history [2]. Not everyone who inherits the gene change linked to Fuchs dystrophy goes on to develop it [5].

## What are the symptoms?

Many people have no symptoms at first, and Fuchs dystrophy is often found at a routine eye test [2].

- **Misty vision when you wake up,** which clears during the day. This is the typical early symptom [2].
- **Glare,** which can be troublesome at night, for example when driving [2].
- **Blurred vision that lasts all day,** as the condition gets worse [2].
- **Pain,** in more advanced cases. Tiny blisters can form on the surface of the cornea, and can cause sharp pain if they burst [1, 2].

## What can I do?

- **If you have no symptoms,** no treatment is needed [3].

- **Salty (hypertonic saline) eye drops or ointment** draw fluid out of the cornea [3, 5]. They may help reduce morning blurring [5], but trials have not shown that they make a meaningful difference to symptoms [4]. Ask your eye team whether they are worth trying for you.
- **Lubricating eye drops** may make the eye more comfortable [1, 4].
- **Sunglasses or tinted glasses** can help if glare is a problem [4].
- **If blisters cause pain,** a soft "bandage" contact lens can protect the surface of the eye [4].

## Cataract surgery and Fuchs dystrophy

Fuchs dystrophy and cataract are separate conditions. But cataract surgery can speed up the loss of endothelial cells, so it can make Fuchs dystrophy worse, even when the operation goes smoothly [2, 4].

- **If your Fuchs dystrophy is mild,** the cornea usually clears after cataract surgery without needing a transplant [2].
- **If it is more advanced,** your surgeon may plan cataract surgery and a corneal transplant together, on the same day or at different times [2].
- In a Spanish study of people with moderate Fuchs dystrophy, about {{risk.cornea.fuchs.transplant_after_cataract}} eyes went on to need a corneal transplant after cataract surgery, most of them within six months [6]. Measurements of the cornea before surgery can help your surgeon judge your own risk [4, 6].

**If you have Fuchs dystrophy, make sure the person referring you for cataract surgery, and your cataract surgeon, know about it** [1, 4].

## Corneal transplant

If Fuchs dystrophy is affecting your vision a lot, or your eye is painful, a corneal transplant may be recommended. It has a good chance of improving your vision and making the eye comfortable [1].

The usual operation replaces only the damaged inner layer of the cornea with a thin layer from a donor. This is called an endothelial keratoplasty (EK), and there are two types, DMEK and DSAEK [4, 7]. There is a separate leaflet on endothelial keratoplasty, which explains the operation, recovery and risks.

- Fuchs dystrophy does not come back in the new layer, although a repeat transplant is sometimes needed [2].
- In UK national data, about {{risk.cornea.transplant.survival_5y_fed}} first transplants for Fuchs dystrophy were still working after five years [8].

## Newer treatments

A procedure that removes the damaged central cells without a donor transplant (Descemet stripping only, or DSO), sometimes followed by eye drops called Rho kinase inhibitors, is being studied [3, 9, 10]. Early results are promising, but it is not yet a routine treatment [3, 9].

## When to get help

Contact us if your eye becomes painful, as this can mean a blister on the surface of the cornea has burst [1, 2], or if your vision is getting worse.

If you have had a corneal transplant, contact us straight away if you have a red eye, eye pain, sensitivity to light or blurred vision [11]. There is more about this in the endothelial keratoplasty leaflet.
